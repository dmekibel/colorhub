# The core insight from my field

Statistics' central contribution to this problem is a discipline most color tools skip entirely: the difference between a measurement and a claim. A palette extracted from one painting is a measurement. "Monet uses blue 8x more than his peers" is a claim, and claims need a null hypothesis, a comparison population, a sample size, and an accounting for the ways the measurement itself is biased (varnish, museum photography, which museums happened to collect which painters). Every competing tool — Adobe Color, Coolors, Khroma — treats a palette as a free-standing aesthetic object with no reference frame. What my field adds is the reference frame: distinctiveness is always relative to a baseline, uncertainty shrinks a thin sample's claims before they embarrass you, and "beautiful" or "unique" are things you can actually operationalize as read-offs from a distribution rather than vibes. The data in hand (837-1,472 painters depending on build, decades, countries, movements, with painter nested inside movement nested inside century) is a textbook hierarchical structure, and almost nothing in competing tools exploits hierarchy at all.

## System 1: Lift-ranked palette extraction (statistically honest "signature colors")

**Pitch:** Don't show a painter's most-area colors — show the colors that are *theirs*, measured against a shrunk baseline.

**Mechanism:** This already exists partially (`artists/<slug>.json` signature colors with lift ≥ 1.15, support ≥ 3% share and ≥2 paintings), but it can go further with empirical Bayes shrinkage. For a painter with n paintings and a candidate color/name c, let raw lift L_c = (painter's mean share of c) / (peer baseline share of c), where peer baseline = decade+country, falling back to country, falling back to archive, with the painter's own paintings excluded (already done, correctly). The problem: with n=7 (a thin painter), L_c is a noisy ratio of a small sample — one accent-heavy painting can produce a 4x lift that's really sampling noise. Fix: James-Stein / Beta-Binomial shrinkage. Model each painting's presence/absence (or binned share) of color c as Binomial(trials=1, p_true), put a Beta(α,β) prior fit from the *peer population's* distribution of that color's share (method of moments over all painters in the peer group), and report the posterior mean shrunk lift L_c_shrunk = (painter_count_c + α) / (n + α + β) divided by the baseline rate. As n grows, shrinkage → raw lift; as n shrinks, lift regresses toward 1.0 (no claim). Expose the credible interval, not just a point estimate: a painter with n=6 gets a wide interval, and the UI shows a wobble/width cue instead of a false-precision single number.

**Data used:** `data/analysis/artists/<slug>.json` signature/avoided color lists and their `support` counts; `data/analysis/groups.json` byDecade/byCountry for baseline rates; need painting-count n per painter, already present. Missing: the raw per-color presence counts needed to refit a Beta prior properly (currently only the final lift number ships) — would need `tools/analyze.py` to also emit, per name, the peer-group's distribution of per-painting share (mean + variance), not just the mean, so a prior can be fit. That's a small addition to the existing artist build, not new data collection.

**What the user sees:** On a painter page, instead of "Signature colors: Ink, Bistre," each signature color carries a confidence band shown as a soft halo width, or just three tiers ("strongly his," "likely his," "worth a grain of salt") — never a bare "2.4x" for a painter with 7 works. Example: Sargent (n=37, a well-populated painter) keeps a tight, confident "Ink, 2.4x" claim; a painter at the n=6 floor with an apparent 3x lift on some rare pigment gets the same number shown with a visibly wider, more tentative presentation, with copy like "early read, more paintings would sharpen this."

**Self-critique:** This is more honest but risks feeling like hedging theater — a width indicator nobody reads, dragging down the "calm surface" rule. It can also be gamed by the varnish confound: colors that survive varnishing differently (blues yellow, blacks stay black) will show spuriously high "signature" status for any painter whose peer group happens to include more or less varnish-aged work, which correlates with century, which correlates with decade baseline — so the lift is partly measuring *age of paint*, not painter intent.

**Improved version:** Stratify the Beta prior not just by decade+country but add a varnish-adjustment term: estimate each decade's expected L*/C* shift from the century trend already computed (`century trend` in `index.json`) and de-mean blue/yellow shares by the century's known aging curve before computing lift, so a painter isn't "distinctive for blue" merely for painting in a less-yellowed decade. Keep the confidence cue but make it a single calm design element (e.g., a color swatch with a slightly desaturated/dotted border under low confidence) rather than a number, satisfying both honesty and calm.

## System 2: The "distinctiveness × beauty" palette calculator (directly answers David's ask)

**Pitch:** A palette-selection engine that doesn't average a painter's work — it searches for the subset of colors that is simultaneously rare (for the painter, movement, or archive) and internally harmonious, at a count the user chooses (3-20).

**Mechanism:** This is exactly David's "most beautiful, most unique, variable count" request, formalized as a combinatorial optimization. Define, for painter P with swatch book S (already built: `data/games/oo/`, area-weighted OKLab k-means swatch book per painter), a scoring function over any subset K ⊂ S of size k:

Score(K) = w1 · Distinctiveness(K) + w2 · Harmony(K) + w3 · Coverage(K) − w4 · Redundancy(K)

- **Distinctiveness(K)** = mean over colors in K of the hue-lift vector already computed (`oo_collections.py`'s HUE LIFT: vivid-area share per 30° hue bin vs the whole archive) — this is the "doesn't feel generic" term. Use log-lift so outliers don't dominate: Σ log(lift_i) / k.
- **Harmony(K)** = a hue-circle tension score, not naive complementary-only: compute pairwise angular distances in OKLab hue between all colors in K, and reward configurations whose distribution matches known aesthetic templates (complementary ~150-210°, split-complementary, analogous clusters + one outlier) via a template-fit likelihood — essentially a mixture model over Itten/Matsuda templates already partially implemented as `glHarmonyFamily`/`harmony-fit heuristic`, but applied here as an objective to *search over*, not just classify after the fact.
- **Coverage(K)** = how much of the painter's actual measured chroma-weighted area the subset's nearest-neighbor Voronoi cells capture (so the palette isn't just five accent flecks nobody would recognize as "him") — this is the honesty anchor; it's why David's reference palettes "feel like him" even though they privilege character colors: they still cover real area, just not *proportionally*.
- **Redundancy(K)** penalizes two colors within OKLab ΔE < some threshold (no two near-identical blues).
- Search: k is small (3-20) and |S| is at most ~95 (Monet) to ~300ish for huge painters, so exact subset search is infeasible for larger k, but a greedy farthest-point-first seed (this already exists as "Diverse") followed by simulated annealing or a simple local-search swap (try replacing each member with every unused swatch, keep if Score improves, repeat until convergence) converges fast — under a second for k ≤ 20, |S| ≤ 300. Expose k as the existing 3/6/12/20 slider, but unlocked to any value in between, since "variable count, not just limited to 5" was explicit.

**Data used:** `data/games/oo/` swatch books, hue-lift vectors, palette families (per-painter); `data/analysis/paintings-NNN.json` harmony-fit labels for calibrating the template-fit likelihoods; `data/gallery/d/NNN.json` 24-color pools for coverage computation per sampled painting. Nothing new needed — this is a genuinely novel combination of three systems David's team already built but never combined into one objective function.

**What the user sees:** A "Most beautiful & unique" toggle next to the existing By-area/Diverse/Stand-out/Harmonies modes, with the familiar count slider now continuous. Tap deeper: "why these five" shows the three component scores as plain sentences ("more distinctive than 90% of his other palettes," "forms a blue-orange tension," "still covers 61% of his canvas"). Example: for van Gogh, this should surface something structurally like the three references David already pointed to — a deep anchor, a blue/yellow-orange tension, each color visibly a different hue family — but *derived*, not copied from his reference palettes, and the honest caveat stays: "chosen for character, not area; this is not how much of the canvas each color actually covers."

**Self-critique:** Worst failure mode: this optimizes exactly the thing David described from three examples, so it is at high risk of overfitting to Van Gogh's late, colorful period and underperforming on painters who genuinely don't have a Van-Gogh-shaped palette — a muted painter like Whistler run through this objective will either produce something fake-looking (forcing tension where there isn't real tension) or will just return his most-saturated-but-still-muted work, disappointing the user expecting "vivid." Also gameable: hue-lift rewards *rare* hues, so a painter with one weird greenish accident in one painting could get that color over-weighted as "distinctive" when it's really a varnish artifact or a restoration patch.

**Improved version:** Add a per-painter ceiling check before offering this mode at all — compute the painter's own archive-wide chroma percentile; if a painter's most-vivid cluster is still low-chroma in absolute terms (e.g., below the archive median), relabel the feature from "Most vivid & unique" to "Most distinctive" and drop vividness language, so Whistler gets an honest "his most characteristic tension" instead of a forced "vivid" claim. Also require minimum support (≥2 paintings contain a near-identical color, OKLab ΔE<5) before a swatch is eligible for K at all, directly killing the one-accident-pixel failure mode.

## System 3: Hierarchical Bayesian palette model (painter < movement < century) for "how unusual is this, really?"

**Pitch:** Every percentile claim in the app currently compares a painting/painter to one flat baseline. A proper hierarchical model lets you ask "unusual for him, or unusual for anyone in 1888" at the same time, with partial pooling solving the small-n problem architecturally instead of case-by-case.

**Mechanism:** Model each painting's log-chroma (or any scalar feature, e.g. vivid-share) as:

y_ijk = μ + α_i (painter) + β_j (movement) + γ_k (century) + ε

with α_i ~ N(0, σ_painter²), β_j ~ N(0, σ_movement²), γ_k ~ N(0, σ_century²), fit via a simple variational or Gibbs approximation (doesn't need Stan — a closed-form empirical Bayes/ANOVA-style variance-components estimate works fine at this sample size, e.g. restricted maximum likelihood). This is a real multilevel model: a painter with only 6 paintings gets their α_i shrunk hard toward their movement's β_j, while Sargent (37) or Rubens (209) get α_i that's almost entirely their own signal. This replaces the current ad hoc fallback chain ("decade+country, falling back to country, falling back to archive") with a principled, continuous version of the same idea — no more sharp discontinuities where a painter who has 5 paintings in one bucket suddenly gets compared to a completely different population than a painter with 6.

**Data used:** `data/analysis/paintings-NNN.json` (L*, C*, warm fraction per painting), `data/analysis/groups.json` byMovement, painter metadata's movement field, decade. Needs the century/decade/movement crossing to actually be computable per painting (already present per painting via artist metadata movement + year). Gap: movement is AIC-only / Wikidata-sparse per the corpus docs — this model would silently assign movement-less paintings (most of Commons) to a "no movement" pseudo-level, which needs an honest label ("movement unknown for this one," not folded into "Unknown" as if it were its own school).

**What the user sees:** Nothing new as a UI surface by itself — this is plumbing that makes every existing percentile claim ("darker than 90% of his work") more honest at the edges (small painters, sparse movements), and it lets a future feature ask two-level questions: "unusual for Impressionism" vs "unusual for him personally," shown as two small bars rather than one. Example: a Berthe Morisot painting that's dark for an Impressionist would show both "darker than 85% of Impressionist paintings" and, separately and not confused with it, "typical for her own work" — a claim the current flat-baseline system can't cleanly separate.

**Self-critique:** Invisible infrastructure work is a hard sell to ship on its own, and variance-component models are easy to get wrong silently (a bad σ estimate shrinks everything toward the grand mean and every painter starts looking falsely average). Also: movement data sparsity means the movement level is built on a biased, AIC-only subsample, so β_j isn't "Impressionism," it's "Impressionism, as sampled by one Chicago museum."

**Improved version:** Ship it paired with System 1 (so there's a visible payoff: tighter, more honest signature-color confidence for thin painters) and expose the model's own uncertainty about σ_movement as a blunt caveat wherever movement claims are made: "based on AIC's movement tags only — most of the archive isn't labeled this way."

## System 4: Permutation-tested "palette fingerprint" distance — painter similarity you can trust

**Pitch:** "Nearest painters by palette" already exists (Euclidean distance on z-scored 6-dim feature vectors) — but Euclidean distance on means says nothing about whether the similarity is statistically real or just two painters with wide, overlapping, uninformative palettes.

**Mechanism:** Replace/augment the point-estimate nearest-neighbor with a permutation test: for painter A and painter B, pool all their paintings together, repeatedly (2,000 times) randomly reassign labels, recompute the same between-painter distance each time to build a null distribution, and report where the real distance falls (a p-value / percentile) — this tells you "closer than 95% of same-sized random splits" rather than just "distance = 0.46." Equivalently and more efficient: bootstrap each painter's own centroid (resample their n paintings with replacement, recompute centroid, 1,000 times) to get a confidence ellipse per painter in feature space, then report overlap of the two ellipses rather than a bare point distance — directly solves the "nearest painter, n=6" problem, since a thin painter's centroid will have a huge ellipse and most "nearest painter" claims for them should visibly deflate in confidence.

**Data used:** The existing 6-dim z-scored feature vectors per painter (`artists/<slug>.json`), raw per-painting feature values needed for resampling (already computed as intermediate values in `tools/analyze.py`, just not currently shipped at the per-painting granularity needed — would need the per-painting feature vector array, not just the painter's final mean, for painters below some n threshold specifically for this bootstrap, which is a modest addition).

**What the user sees:** "Nearest painters by palette" gets an honesty qualifier based on n: Sargent (n=37) → "paints most like José Casado del Alisal" stated plainly; a 6-painting painter → "paints somewhat like X, though with only 6 works this is a loose match" with the UI visibly softer (lighter weight type, maybe literally blurred edges on the match card). Example from the existing Sargent data: his current nearest-list (Casado del Alisal 0.46, Rosales 0.50...) at n=37 is probably stable; the system would confirm this with a tight bootstrap ellipse and keep the confident phrasing, while a thin painter's "nearest" might turn out to not even beat random relabeling, in which case the honest move is to suppress the claim entirely rather than show a p=0.4 "nearest painter."

**Self-critique:** Adds real compute cost (2,000 permutations × 1,472 painters pairwise is expensive if not short-circuited) and a user who wants a fun fact doesn't want a p-value lecture. Also, feature-vector distance using only 6 summary dimensions throws away the actual shape of a palette (two painters could have identical means and totally different hue distributions).

**Improved version:** Only run the bootstrap/permutation check for painters below n=15 (where it actually changes the answer) — above that, the point estimate is already stable, so this is cheap in practice. Translate output to one of three tiers in copy (confident / tentative / suppressed) rather than exposing any number, and add one more real dimension to the feature vector cheaply available from the hue-lift vector (a 12-bin hue-histogram cosine similarity) so "nearest" captures hue *shape*, not just mean L*/C*.

## System 5: Change-point detection upgraded to a proper regime-switching model, with uncertainty on the breakpoint itself

**Pitch:** The current change-point rule (biggest single decade-to-decade jump ≥8 in mean L*) is a threshold heuristic that can't say how confident it is in *when* the shift happened, or whether there even was a single shift vs a gradual drift vs two shifts.

**Mechanism:** Replace the single-biggest-jump rule with a real changepoint algorithm — PELT (Pruned Exact Linear Time) or a simple Bayesian changepoint model over the painter's own time series of per-painting (not per-decade-mean) chroma/L* — because binning into decades before detecting a change discards exactly the information (which paintings, which year) needed to locate it precisely. Model: a piecewise-constant mean with unknown number and location of breakpoints, penalized by BIC (classic PELT objective: minimize Σ[cost of each segment] + λ·(number of breakpoints), cost = within-segment sum of squared deviation from its mean, λ tuned so spurious breakpoints from single-painting noise don't get flagged — set λ ≥ some multiple of the archive's painting-to-painting variance floor). Output isn't just "the change happens around the 1890s" but a probability-weighted location (e.g., a small distribution over years, visualized as a soft gradient rather than a hard line) plus a magnitude and direction.

**Data used:** `data/analysis/artists/<slug>.json` palette-by-decade already computed; needs the underlying per-painting time series (year + L*/C*) which exists in `data/gallery/d/NNN.json` per painting (year field) joined against `data/analysis/paintings-NNN.json`. This is a straightforward re-derivation from existing per-painting records, not new data.

**What the user sees:** Van Gogh's career page could show a literal timeline strip (year on x-axis, L*/chroma as a smoothed line with soft confidence band) with one or two shaded regions marking detected regime changes, replacing or augmenting the current "change-point flagged at the 1890s" sentence for Sargent-type examples with something that shows its work: here's the actual trajectory, here's where the data says something shifted, here's how sure we are. For Van Gogh specifically (202 paintings, strong undated-year caveats aside), this is the single best candidate in the whole archive to demonstrate the method, since his documented Paris→Arles→Saint-Rémy color shift is real art history the model should recover and the user will recognize, which is exactly the kind of result that builds trust in every other statistical claim in the app.

**Self-critique:** Van Gogh's dates are well-documented so this will likely work beautifully and build false confidence that it'll work everywhere — most painters have sparser, more irregularly-dated samples where year gaps of 5-10 years between measured paintings make any changepoint claim close to meaningless, and the model has no way to represent "we have no paintings between 1850 and 1865" except a suspiciously flat interpolation.

**Improved version:** Gate the whole feature on a minimum temporal density check (e.g., require at least N paintings per decade across the painter's active span, or explicit coverage gaps shown as literal blank/hatched regions on the timeline rather than interpolated lines) — a changepoint claim should be visually impossible to show across a 15-year documentation gap, full stop, not just caveated in prose nobody reads.

## System 6: Confound-adjusted "true palette" estimate (de-varnishing as a statistical, not pixel, operation)

**Pitch:** Every honest caveat in this app says "these are photographs of varnished paintings" and stops there. Statistics can go one step further: model and partially remove the varnish/age confound as a regression adjustment, clearly labeled as a model output, not a measurement.

**Mechanism:** The century trend (`index.json`) already shows mean L* rising over centuries within the same regions/genres — part of this is real stylistic change (Europe genuinely lightened), part is varnish yellowing/darkening older works more. These are hard to fully separate without ground truth, but a partial identification strategy exists: use paintings with *known conservation/cleaning dates or recent restoration* (if any museum metadata flags this — needs checking; likely a gap) as a quasi-control group, or, more feasibly with current data, use the within-painter decade trend itself: if a painter's individual palette shift across decades correlates with the *archive-wide* century drift at the same rate as everyone else, that shared component is suspect as confound; the painter-specific *residual* after removing the archive's own century-level drift (γ_k from System 3's hierarchical model) is a better estimate of real artistic change. Concretely: Δ_painter = (painter's observed L* in decade d) − (archive-wide expected L* in decade d from the century trend) — this is just removing γ_k, already a byproduct of System 3's model, applied here as a "varnish-adjusted trajectory" overlay.

**Data used:** Century trend in `index.json`, per-painter palette-by-decade. Missing: real restoration/conservation-date metadata, which none of the seven museum sources ship in the current pipeline (would need to check AIC/Met/NGA records for a "conservation" or "exhibition history" field — likely sparse to nonexistent, so this stays a residual-based statistical adjustment, not a true de-confound, and must be labeled as such).

**What the user sees:** An optional "adjusted for the archive's own aging trend" toggle on a painter's decade-trajectory view — off by default (the calm, literal, "as photographed" reading), on for people who want the deeper, hedged layer. Example: Rembrandt's famously dark palette partly reflects 17th-century varnish on top of 17th-century varnish on top of real Dutch tenebrism — this system would show both the raw trajectory and a "if the whole archive's own 1600s-vs-now drift is removed" adjusted line, with the gap between them presented as "how much of the darkness might be age, estimated, not measured."

**Self-critique:** This is the highest-risk idea in the set for exactly the reason the app's honesty rules exist: a "de-varnished" estimate looks authoritative and will get screenshotted and believed as fact, when it is actually a model residual built on an assumption (that painter-level and archive-level aging drift are separable and share a common cause) that could easily be wrong — a painter who deliberately got darker over their career will have that real choice partly subtracted out as if it were just varnish.

**Improved version:** Never call it "de-varnished" or "the real colors" anywhere in copy — call it "the archive-wide aging trend" as an overlay line on the same chart as the raw data, both visible at once, with the raw line as the default truth and the adjusted line explicitly labeled as "one hypothesis, not a correction" with a one-tap explainer. Possibly kill this one for v1 and revisit only if a historian on the panel confirms there's real art-historical value in separating the two, since the risk/value ratio is the worst of anything here.

## System 7: Palette "surprise" via information-theoretic entropy, separating boring-but-balanced from boring-but-monotone

**Pitch:** "Diverse" (farthest-point-in-OKLab) and "distinctive" (lift-based) both exist, but neither captures a third, genuinely different axis: how *predictable* a painting's palette is given its painter's/movement's/decade's typical palette — a cross-entropy or KL-divergence surprise score.

**Mechanism:** Build a reference distribution over hue bins (the hue histogram already computed per painting, `hh` field, or the painter-level hue-lift vector) for the relevant population (painter, movement, or decade). For a given painting's own hue histogram P and the reference Q, compute KL(P‖Q) = Σ p_i log(p_i/q_i), or symmetrized Jensen-Shannon divergence for numerical stability with small bin counts and zero bins. This is a genuinely different signal from "vivid" or "distinctive color list": a painting can use completely ordinary, un-lifted colors in an unusual *proportion* (e.g., a painter who's normally balanced doing one painting that's 80% a single hue) and this would flag it as surprising while lift-based signature-color detection would miss it entirely (lift looks at colors present/absent, not distributional shape).

**Data used:** `data/analysis/paintings-NNN.json` per-painting hue histogram (`hh`, base64-packed, already computed); painter/movement/decade-level aggregate hue histograms (derivable from existing per-painting records, or already summarized in `groups.json` top/dist colors, though the raw histogram shape may need re-deriving from per-painting records since groups.json currently ships named-color lists, not full hue histograms — a modest addition).

**What the user sees:** A "most surprising paintings" view per painter — distinct from "most atypical" (Euclidean distance on summary features, already shipped) because it's measuring shape-of-distribution surprise, which can catch things the mean-based atypicality metric misses. Example: among Monet's 193 paintings, this might surface a painting that isn't necessarily his lightest or darkest (so wouldn't top the existing "atypical" list) but concentrates its color into an unusually narrow hue band for him — described honestly as "unusually concentrated in [hue family] for a Monet, by the shape of its color distribution, not just its brightness."

**Self-critique:** Entropy/KL-divergence on a 12-bin histogram from a single painting's 24-color pool is a genuinely small-sample estimate — one undersampled painting with a quirky 24-color extraction could show spuriously high divergence just from quantization noise, not real artistic surprise, and users will not intuitively understand what "KL-divergence" means even translated to English, risking a feature that's mathematically interesting but communicates nothing.

**Improved version:** Smooth the per-painting histogram with a Dirichlet prior pooled from the painter's own other works (same shrinkage idea as System 1, applied to a distribution instead of a scalar) before computing divergence, killing the small-sample noise problem, and translate the output purely into relative language ("more hue-concentrated than 95% of his other work") rather than ever showing "KL=0.34" — the number itself never surfaces, only its percentile rank within the relevant population, which is a format the app already uses successfully elsewhere (archive/painter percentiles).

## System 8: A/B-tested, not assumed, beauty model — closing the loop on "most beautiful"

**Pitch:** Every system above that touches "beautiful" (System 2 especially) currently has to guess at component weights (w1...w4) from first principles. Statistics' actual job here is to not guess forever — instrument the "Most beautiful & unique" feature itself as a live experiment and fit the weights from real user behavior (which generated palettes get saved, lingered on, or re-rolled away from).

**Mechanism:** Treat Score(K) = w1·Distinctiveness + w2·Harmony + w3·Coverage − w4·Redundancy from System 2 as a linear model with unknown weights. Log, per generated palette shown, the four component scores plus a binary engagement outcome (saved / shared / shuffled-away within N seconds / lingered). Fit a logistic regression (or even simpler, a Bradley-Terry pairwise-preference model if the UI ever offers "pick A or B," which is cheap to add) predicting engagement from the components, periodically refit (weekly batch, no need for real-time ML infra) and feed the fitted weights back into System 2's objective — this closes the loop between "we guessed harmony matters 1.5x as much as coverage" and what users actually respond to, without needing any new infrastructure beyond event logging the app likely already wants for other reasons (taste model, `js/tastemodel.js`, already exists and could absorb this).

**Data used:** New: interaction logs per generated palette (doesn't exist yet — needs instrumentation, which is cheap: log the 4 component scores + outcome per palette shown, client-side, aggregated). Existing: `js/tastemodel.js`/`js/prefmodel.js` as the natural home for this, since they already model user preference.

**What the user sees:** Nothing different on day one — the payoff is that System 2's palettes quietly get better over weeks/months as real engagement data accumulates, the same way a recommender system improves. Eventually, if volume supports it, this could even personalize the weights per user (someone who always keeps the muted, low-harmony-tension palettes gets a model nudged that way) — but that's a second-order feature, not v1.

**Self-critique:** Needs real traffic volume to mean anything — if only a few hundred people ever touch this feature, the regression will be noisy and risks confidently updating weights off nothing. Also, "saved" or "lingered" are weak proxies for "beautiful" — a palette might get saved because it's useful for a specific project, not because it's beautiful, conflating two different user intents.

**Improved version:** Don't ship this until there's a baseline estimate of expected weekly interaction volume with the feature; until then, ship System 2 with hand-set weights and simply log the four components unconditionally from day one (cheap, no behavior change) so that whenever volume justifies it, the historical data already exists to fit retroactively instead of waiting another few months after deciding to build this.

## System 9: Multiple-comparisons-aware "distinctive color" surfacing, archive-wide

**Pitch:** With ~1,000 learnable names × 1,472 painters × multiple comparison baselines, the app is implicitly running tens of thousands of lift tests. Nothing currently corrects for the fact that at this volume, some "distinctive" colors are distinctive purely by chance (multiple-comparisons / false-discovery problem) — exactly the kind of error statisticians exist to catch.

**Mechanism:** Apply Benjamini-Hochberg false-discovery-rate control across each painter's full battery of per-name lift tests before anything is labeled "signature," rather than a single fixed lift/support threshold applied name-by-name in isolation. For painter P tested against ~1,000 names, rank all p-values (derivable from the Beta-Binomial posterior in System 1, or a simple bootstrap p-value on the lift), and keep only names where p_i ≤ (i/m)·q for FDR level q (e.g., 0.1) — this means a painter tested against many names needs correspondingly stronger evidence for any single "signature color" claim to survive, which is a real, well-known correction the current simple-threshold approach doesn't apply at all.

**Data used:** Same as System 1 — artist lift lists, with the correction applied as a post-processing step across the *full* candidate list per painter (which may require the build to retain more candidate colors per painter internally than currently ship, since the correction needs to see all ~1,000 tests, not just the ones that already passed a fixed threshold).

**What the user sees:** No visible UI change — this is a quieter, more defensible version of the existing "Signature colors" list, likely trimming a few borderline entries that were lift-qualifying by chance alone, especially for painters tested against the full name list with many works. Example: a mid-sample painter currently showing three "signature" colors at lift 1.2-1.3 might, after FDR correction, keep only one if the other two don't survive multiple-testing correction — a strictly more honest list, occasionally shorter.

**Self-critique:** Totally invisible to a non-technical reviewer and easy to skip under deadline pressure since nothing breaks without it — it's the kind of correctness fix that only matters when someone screenshots a false "signature color" claim and an art historian calls it out. Low glamour, real risk if skipped.

**Improved version:** Bundle this as a required part of System 1's shrinkage build rather than a separate feature — ship them together so it's not optional engineering debt that gets dropped.

## System 10: Movement/decade "centroid drift" as an animated, honest macro-narrative

**Pitch:** Turn the century-trend numbers that already exist into the statistically correct version of a "evolution of color in art" timeline — a literal animated centroid path through color space across centuries, with confidence ellipses, rather than a misleadingly smooth curve.

**Mechanism:** Already partially computed (century trend, mean L*/vivid-share). Extend to a full 2D (or 3D with hue) centroid-per-century path through OKLab space, each century's point sized by n and shown with a bootstrap confidence ellipse (resample paintings within century, recompute centroid, repeat 500x, plot the 90% ellipse). This directly visualizes sample-size honesty: the 1100s (n=35) gets a huge ellipse, the 1800s (n=5,186) a tiny tight one — the uncertainty is the visualization, not an asterisk in a caveat paragraph.

**What the user sees:** A single scrollable/animated "color through time" strip on an "about the archive" or exploratory page — a calm, beautiful macro view that's also, structurally, a statistics lesson (uncertainty visibly shrinks as data grows) without ever using the word "confidence interval." Example: the strip would visibly show the 1100s-1300s ellipses overlapping heavily (small, Asia-dominated samples per the region table) while 1600s-on separates cleanly — directly surfacing the real sampling bias (Europe/Asia mix shifts by century) as a visual fact instead of hiding it in prose.

**Self-critique:** Risks becoming exactly the kind of "colors evolved from dull to vivid, isn't that interesting" pop-sci narrative the honesty rules exist to prevent — a viewer will read a left-to-right brightening path as "art got more colorful over time" (partly real) without absorbing that it's also "the sample mix changed and varnish ages unevenly" (also real, and inseparable from the first with this data).

**Improved version:** Force the region-mix caveat into the same visual frame, not a separate caveat line — e.g., color the ellipse's fill by the century's Europe/Asia/Americas mix (already tabulated) so the confound is literally visible in the same glance as the trend, not something a reader has to separately remember to discount.

## My one wild idea: a full generative model of "a painter's palette" as a latent process, with posterior-predictive synthesis

Taken to its logical conclusion, my field's real contribution isn't any single feature — it's modeling the *process* that generates a painter's palettes, not just summarizing the palettes that exist. Imagine a hierarchical generative model: each painter has a latent "palette style" — a point (or small manifold) in some low-dimensional color-strategy space, parameterized by things like typical hue-tension angle, typical chroma range, typical value-structure (dark-anchor vs light-anchor), and typical family-diversity — and each actual painting is a noisy draw conditioned on that latent style plus a decade-specific drift term (from System 3/5's hierarchical model) plus a subject-specific perturbation (portraits vs landscapes pull differently). Fit this across all 1,472 painters jointly (a painter's latent style is itself drawn from their movement's latent style, which is drawn from the century's — full hierarchy, shared statistical strength flowing all the way down to the thinnest painters). Once fit, you can do things no competing tool can touch: generate a *posterior-predictive* palette — "what would a painting by this painter, that doesn't exist, plausibly look like in color" — not as a fabricated fake painting, but as an honestly-labeled statistical extrapolation ("a palette statistically consistent with his style, not a real painting's palette"); interpolate between two painters' latent styles to get "a palette halfway between Monet and Hokusai" with an actual probabilistic basis instead of just blending two color lists; or detect when a painter's latent style estimate is unstable (wide posterior) vs crisp, making the uncertainty-reporting from Systems 1/4 a natural byproduct of one unified model instead of six separate bolted-on confidence hacks. This is the single idea that would make competitors' tools look like toys, because it's not pattern-matching on existing palettes — it's a real probabilistic theory of how painters make color choices, fit from 42,331 real data points, honestly uncertain where the data is thin, and generative rather than purely descriptive. It's also a multi-month research project, not a sprint, and would need the painter/web-designer/art-historian panel to sanity-check that the latent "style space" dimensions actually correspond to something a human would recognize as "style" rather than a statistically convenient but meaningless axis.
