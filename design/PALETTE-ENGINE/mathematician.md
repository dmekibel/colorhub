# The Mathematician's Brief: Palettes as Geometric and Combinatorial Objects

## The core insight from my field

Every palette tool on the market treats a palette as a *list* — an ordered tuple of five or six hex codes, chosen by some ad hoc heuristic (k-means centroids, "pick the top N by area," a hand-picked curator). What my field actually knows is that a palette is better modeled as a **point in a much richer space**: a probability distribution over a 3-manifold (OKLab or CIELAB restricted to the sRGB gamut), a subset selected from a ground set under a *submodular* objective, a path through a metric space traced by a career or a century, or a simplex whose vertices are the colors and whose volume is a computable, comparable number. Once you accept that, three things fall out that no competitor currently exploits:

1. **"Beauty" and "uniqueness" are optimization objectives, not vibes.** If you can write down a scoring function over subsets of colors — one term for perceptual spread, one for harmony-template fit, one for rarity against a reference distribution — then the "best" palette of size *k* is the *argmax* of that function, and you can compute it, not guess at it with k-means. This is the single biggest gap in every competitor from Adobe Color to Khroma: they all *cluster* (find typical colors) when the user usually wants *coverage* or *contrast* (find colors that together say something), which is a fundamentally different combinatorial problem (facility location / k-means vs. max-dispersion / submodular coverage).
2. **Variable color count is not a UI slider bolted onto a fixed algorithm — it is a nested sequence that a correctly chosen objective produces for free.** Many good selection objectives are *monotone submodular*, which means there is a canonical, mathematically principled way to grow a palette from 1 color to 20 colors such that the 3-color version is literally a subset of the 6-color version, which is a subset of the 12-color version. This is the "rate-distortion curve" / "persistence diagram" idea: instead of running k-means six times with k=3,6,12,20, you run **one** greedy algorithm once and read off every prefix.
3. **A painter's whole career is a trajectory in color-space, not a bag of paintings.** Treat each painting as a point (or a small distribution) in a 20–40 dimensional "palette feature space" (hue histogram, chroma stats, value key, harmony-fit vector). A career becomes a **curve** through that space, parametrized by time. Then all of differential geometry, time-series statistics, and dynamical-systems change-point detection becomes available: velocity (how fast the palette is changing), curvature (is the change accelerating, like late Monet), and recurrence (does the painter return to earlier color regions, like Picasso's "blue period" echoes). No competitor models a career as a curve; they model it as a folder of images.

With that frame, here are the systems.

## 1. The Dispersion Palette — replacing k-means with max-min diversity

**Pitch:** stop clustering for "typical," start optimizing for "spread." The palette that actually captures what a painting or a painter *contains*, at any color count, chosen so no two colors are redundant.

**Mechanism.** Given the painting's 24-color pool (`data/gallery/d/<shard>.json` field 10, decoded to OKLab + area share), define the ground set *S* as those 24 (or fewer, after dropping sub-threshold specks) colors. For a target count *k*, the Dispersion Palette is

argmax over P ⊆ S, |P| = k   of   min_{i≠j ∈ P} dE(i, j)

i.e., max-min facility dispersion in OKLab ΔE. This is NP-hard in general but has a classic greedy 2-approximation: start with the two colors farthest apart, then repeatedly add the color maximizing its minimum distance to everything already chosen. That greedy construction is *exactly* the nested-subset property David wants for free: the 3-color, 6-color and 12-color palettes are literally prefixes of one sorted list. You never recompute; you just cut the list at *k*. Weight the objective by area share raised to a small power (share^0.3, not share^1) so a 2%-area cadmium red can still win a slot against three near-identical 15%-area blues — this directly encodes "the colors it actually contains, chosen for character, not area," which is exactly the complaint David made about dull averaging.

This is a strict mathematical upgrade of the existing `glPoolDiverse` (farthest-point in OKLab) — the engine already does greedy farthest-point sampling. The new content is (a) the share^0.3 reweighting so dispersion isn't swamped by big dull fields, (b) a formal nestedness guarantee stated and tested, and (c) extending it from one painting's pool to a *painter's entire swatch book* (the `tools/oo_collections.py` weighted-k-means book, already built for Odd One Out — Monet's 95 swatches, Rembrandt's 60).

**Data used.** `data/gallery/d/NNN.json` pool field (painting level); `data/games/oo/<kind>/<id>.json` → `sw` swatch book (painter/movement/decade level, already built). Nothing missing at painting level. At collection level the swatch book already exists; it just needs this new selection rule applied to it instead of (or alongside) the existing family logic.

**User sees.** On a painting page, the existing 3/6/12/20 slider now moves along one precomputed sorted list instead of four separate computations — strictly faster, and the colors don't jump around between counts (today, if Diverse is recomputed per-k, a color present at k=6 can vanish at k=12; with nesting it never does). On a painter page, a new "Dispersion book" view: "Monet's 12 most different colors, across 193 paintings" with a one-line caveat that this favors rarity over frequency, so very common blues might be underrepresented relative to a "most-used" view sitting right next to it.

**Self-critique.** Pure max-min dispersion is biased toward *outliers*: it will happily spend a slot on a single half-percent fleck of vermilion if nothing else is as far from the rest, which can make the palette feel like a grab-bag of extremes rather than "the vivid, gorgeous essence" David actually wants. It also says nothing about harmony — two maximally-dispersed colors might clash rather than sing.

**Improved version.** Replace plain ΔE dispersion with a **penalized dispersion**: score(P) = min-pairwise-ΔE(P) + λ·harmony-fit(P) − μ·(fraction of P that is sub-1%-area). Harmony-fit scores against Itten/Matsuda templates (already computed per painting as `harmony family label` in `data/analysis/paintings-NNN.json`); the penalty term suppresses the ultra-rare specks unless they're truly structurally important (i.e., unless removing them collapses the harmony fit). This is now a weighted combinatorial objective rather than pure geometry, but still greedy-approximable, still nests.

## 2. The Beauty–Uniqueness Pareto Frontier (the literal "most beautiful, most unique" calculator David asked for)

**Pitch:** instead of one "best" palette, show the whole frontier of beautiful-vs-unusual tradeoffs, and let David pick a point on it.

**Mechanism.** Define two scalar functions on any candidate palette P of k colors drawn from a painting's pool or a painter's swatch book:

- **Beauty(P)** — a weighted sum of: harmony-template fit (how well P's hue histogram matches a canonical harmonic template — complementary, analogous, split-complementary — already computed per painting), value contrast (spread of L* across P, since David's three reference palettes all pair a near-black anchor with a light accent), and a chroma-balance term rewarding *some* high-chroma members without *all* of them (full saturation everywhere reads garish, not beautiful — it's the contrast between a near-black navy and a lemon yellow that reads as beautiful, not uniform vividness).
- **Uniqueness(P)** — KL-divergence (or Hellinger distance, which is already used in `oo_collections.py` for family clustering, so the codebase already has this metric) of P's hue/chroma histogram against the *reference* distribution: the whole-archive histogram, or the painter's-peers histogram, or the painter's-own-average histogram. High uniqueness = "colors this painting/painter uses far more than the baseline."

For a fixed k, enumerate (or beam-search, since the pool is only 24 colors and C(24,6) ≈ 134,596 is trivial to brute-force; C(95,6) for a swatch book needs beam search or a greedy/local-search heuristic) candidate palettes and plot Beauty(P) against Uniqueness(P). The **Pareto frontier** — the set of palettes where no other palette is simultaneously more beautiful *and* more unique — is the real answer to "give me the most beautiful, most unique palette": it isn't one number, it's a *curve*, and the UI can let David slide along it from "safe and gorgeous" to "weird and signature." This formalizes exactly his comment that it's "about harmony and beauty and uniqueness, not about average" — averaging destroys uniqueness by definition (it regresses to the mean), so a Pareto search is the structurally correct inverse of averaging.

**Data used.** Painting pool / painter swatch book (as above) for candidate colors; `data/analysis/paintings-NNN.json`'s harmony-family label and L*/chroma stats for the Beauty term; `data/analysis/artists/<slug>.json`'s signature-colors-with-lift and `data/analysis/groups.json`'s decade/movement baselines for the Uniqueness reference distribution (lift is already basically log-likelihood-ratio against peers, which is most of the Uniqueness term already computed). **Missing:** a validated weighting between the Beauty sub-terms — this needs a small human-preference study (see "what I need from other specialists," the statistician's job) rather than my inventing coefficients from nothing.

**User sees.** On a painter page, "Palette finder" with two handles on one slider: count (3–20) and a Beauty↔Unique dial. Example (describing what it *would* show, not a fabricated number): for van Gogh, dialing toward "most unique" at k=5 should surface palettes drawn from the Arles/Saint-Rémy years — because those are where his hue histogram departs furthest from his own early Dutch-period baseline — without the tool ever being told "pick the colorful years"; the uniqueness math discovers that, which is a much stronger claim than a hardcoded "skip his early palette" rule. Dialing toward "most beautiful" at the same k should favor palettes with one dark anchor and one light accent because that's what the harmony/contrast terms reward, matching the structure of all three of David's references.

**Self-critique.** Beauty(P) is the most editorially fraught scalar in the whole plan — collapsing "is this beautiful" into one weighted sum is exactly the kind of thing that *looks* objective but is actually a hidden value judgment, and if the weights are wrong it will confidently rank a muddy palette as "beautiful" because it hit a harmony-template technicality. It's also expensive: brute-forcing C(n,k) over a 95-color swatch book at k=12 is intractable, and real art may have *no* palette in the data that scores well on both axes (Beauty and Uniqueness can be genuinely anti-correlated for a painter whose unusual work is also their ugliest), leaving an empty or degenerate frontier.

**Improved version.** (a) Make Beauty(P) explicitly a *family* of scoring functions (harmony-only, contrast-only, combined) shown as three thin frontiers rather than one blended line, so the UI is honest about which beauty-definition produced which pick rather than hiding the subjectivity inside one number. (b) Replace brute force with the **greedy submodular approximation** from idea #1 as a candidate generator (dispersion already approximates a form of "interesting coverage"), then do local 1-swap hill-climbing on Beauty+Uniqueness starting from that seed — tractable at any swatch-book size, with a provable approximation bound inherited from submodularity. (c) When the frontier is degenerate (every unique palette is also "ugly" by the beauty score, or vice versa), say so explicitly as a *finding*: "This painter's most distinctive colors don't form a conventionally harmonious group" — which is itself a genuinely interesting, honest, never-seen-before statement about an artist, consistent with the app's "measure, then tell" philosophy.

## 3. Optimal Transport as the native language of "compare any two palettes"

**Pitch:** every comparison in the app — this painting vs. its painter's average, this decade vs. that decade, this movement vs. that movement, even "my photo vs. Monet" — is secretly the same mathematical operation, and it's currently done badly (or not at all) everywhere it appears.

**Mechanism.** Model any palette, any swatch book, or any painting's pool as a discrete probability distribution over OKLab: a set of (color, weight) pairs where weight = area share (or swatch-book weight-per-10,000, already stored). The **earth mover's distance** (Wasserstein-1 metric) between two such distributions,

W(μ,ν) = min over couplings π   Σ π(x,y) · dE(x,y),  subject to π's marginals = μ, ν

is the minimum total "perceptual work" to morph one palette's color mass into the other's. This single number replaces at least four different ad hoc comparisons the app currently wants to make in different places: "how typical is this painting of its collection" (already approximated in `oo_collections.py` via Bhattacharyya overlap of histograms — a reasonable but cruder stand-in), "how similar are two painters" (`nearest painters` in the per-painter analysis), "how far has this decade's palette moved from the last one," and "how close is my own uploaded photo's palette to Sargent's." It is the *right* metric because, unlike Bhattacharyya/Hellinger (which only compare bin *overlap*, treating adjacent bins as totally unrelated), Wasserstein respects the geometry of color space itself — moving mass from "navy" to "cobalt" costs less than moving it to "lemon yellow," which is exactly the perceptual structure color data has and generic histogram distances throw away.

As a bonus, OT doesn't just give you a *distance*, it gives you the **coupling** π — literally, a map of which of painting A's colors "became" which of painting B's colors under the cheapest transformation. That coupling is directly renderable: arrows from one palette's swatches to the other's.

**Data used.** Painting pools (`data/gallery/d/*.json`), painter swatch books and decade/movement swatch books (`data/games/oo/*`), decade/movement/country aggregates (`data/analysis/groups.json`). Computing exact Wasserstein on ground sets of 24–200 points is fast (network-simplex or Sinkhorn-regularized OT solve in milliseconds; this is offline-buildable, fits the static-site constraint). **Nothing missing** — this is purely a better algorithm over data already collected, which makes it one of the highest-ROI, lowest-risk items in this whole document.

**User sees.** A "transport map" between any two collections — e.g., Monet's 1870s palette vs. his 1900s water-lily palette: a small diagram where each 1870s swatch has a thin line to the 1900s swatch it most "became," line thickness = mass moved, with the finding sentence "about 40% of his color mass shifted from mid-value greens toward high-chroma blue-violets" (again — a description of the kind of claim this would generate, not an invented number). Deeper layer: tap any arrow to see the two actual paintings whose colors anchor that flow.

**Self-critique.** Exact OT's "optimal" coupling can be mathematically optimal yet *perceptually nonsensical* in a small, sparse ground set — with only 24–60 points, the solver may route a tiny 0.5%-share fleck through a long, visually weird path just because it's technically cheapest in aggregate, producing a transport-map arrow that looks arbitrary to a human. It's also a comparison tool, not a generation tool — it never, by itself, produces a *new* palette; it only scores or maps existing ones.

**Improved version.** Regularize with entropic OT (Sinkhorn), which smooths the coupling so mass spreads across several plausible matches instead of committing to one brittle optimal path, and is also an order of magnitude faster, trivial to batch-precompute for every painter-pair in the "nearest painters" list. Pair it with #1's dispersion palette as the generator: use OT distance as the *scoring* half of a system where the dispersion/beam-search machinery in #1–2 does the *generating*.

## 4. Rate–distortion curves as the principled answer to "how many colors does this painting even need?"

**Pitch:** a mathematically grounded way to answer "is 6 colors enough, or is this really a 14-color painting?" per artwork — variable count that *means something* rather than being a UI convenience.

**Mechanism.** This is literally the rate-distortion problem from information theory applied to the pool. For a painting's full pixel-area-weighted color distribution (approximated here by the 24-color pool with shares), define distortion D(k) = the minimum achievable Wasserstein distance (idea #3) between the true pool-distribution and *any* k-color summary of it (solved via weighted k-means in OKLab, or by the greedy dispersion construction from #1 — both are reasonable "rate-k encoders"). D(k) is monotonically non-increasing and convex-ish; plot D(k) vs. k and you get exactly the rate-distortion curve of this one painting's color content. The **elbow** of that curve — where D(k) stops dropping fast per additional color — is a principled, computable answer to "how many colors does this painting *really* have," distinct for every painting, instead of the app's current fixed stops of 3/6/12/20.

**Data used.** Painting pool (painting level) or swatch book (collection level). Fully computable offline; no missing data.

**User sees.** The existing count slider gains a small, honest marker: "this painting's content levels off around 9 colors" (again, illustrative, not a fabricated figure) — i.e., past that point, added colors are mostly redundant restatements of ones already shown, which becomes a *finding* in itself: a painting whose elbow sits at 4 colors is measurably simpler/flatter than one whose elbow sits at 16, a genuinely new, archive-wide, comparable statistic no competitor has (Adobe Color's fixed 5-swatch extractor has no notion of "how many colors this image actually needs").

**Self-critique.** The elbow location is sensitive to the distortion metric's units and to the arbitrary pool size cap (24) — a painting with genuinely more than 24 real color regions is truncated before the curve even starts, and "elbow detection" itself is a notoriously underspecified heuristic (several reasonable formal definitions — max second-derivative, max distance-to-chord, knee via Kneedle algorithm — can disagree).

**Improved version.** Report the elbow as a *range* (e.g., via the Kneedle algorithm's own confidence band) rather than a single integer, and always show the full D(k) curve on tap-through (the "deeper layer") so the honest uncertainty is visible rather than hidden behind one falsely precise number — directly satisfying the app's "measure, then tell, with honest caveats" rule.

## 5. Career as a curve: velocity, curvature and recurrence in the painter's own color manifold

**Pitch:** stop treating "palette by decade" as a bar of swatches per decade (already shipped) and start treating a career as a parametrized path, so you can ask calculus questions about it: when did it change fastest, did it ever double back, is it accelerating.

**Mechanism.** Embed every painting of a painter as a feature vector (its hue histogram, mean L*/C*, warm/cool balance — all already in `data/analysis/paintings-NNN.json`) in ℝ^n, reduce to 2–3 dimensions via PCA or UMAP for display, and treat the time-ordered sequence as a discretized curve γ(t). Then: **velocity** |γ'(t)| (via finite differences between adjacent-year or adjacent-decade centroids) locates exactly when a painter's palette was changing fastest — this is a *rigorous* version of the "change point" already computed per painter, but as a continuous signal rather than a single detected breakpoint, so it can show gradual acceleration too. **Curvature** (rate of change of direction, not just magnitude) distinguishes "palette is shifting steadily in one direction" from "palette is churning, trying many things" — two qualitatively different careers that a single change-point number can't tell apart. **Recurrence** — does γ(t) pass near a region of color-space it already visited? — is computable as a self-distance matrix (like a recurrence plot in dynamical systems), and would let the app say something like "this later period's palette sits closer to his earliest work than to his middle period" (a genuinely interesting, previously uncomputed claim) without ever needing a human to have noticed it.

**Data used.** Per-painting stats already in `data/analysis/paintings-NNN.json` (hue histogram, L*/C* stats, warm/cool fraction) plus each painting's year (from `data/gallery/index.json`); painter-level `change point` and `palette by decade` already in `data/analysis/artists/<slug>.json` as a sanity check / simpler baseline to compare against. Nothing new to collect — this is a reanalysis of existing per-painting features as a *sequence* rather than a *bag*.

**User sees.** On a painter page, below the existing decade swatches: a small 2D path ("the color journey") with year labels, a highlighted fast-change segment, and — only if detected — a dotted line connecting two non-adjacent periods that are unusually close in color-space ("revisits"). Example: a Hokusai career curve might show a long, slow drift (velocity low, decades of incremental change) versus a van Gogh curve showing a violent, late, high-velocity swing into the Arles period — exactly the "colorful stage" David pointed at, but *discovered* by the math from the shape of the curve rather than asserted.

**Self-critique.** With under ~20 dated works per decade for many painters, the per-decade centroid is noisy, so velocity/curvature on a sparse, undated-heavy corpus (the dataset's own documented limitation: "many paintings are undated") can manufacture a dramatic-looking "acceleration" that's really just sampling noise from 4 paintings in one bucket and 40 in the next. PCA/UMAP projections are also notoriously unstable and can make two genuinely different trajectories look artificially similar or different depending on the embedding's random seed.

**Improved version.** Weight every per-decade centroid by its painting count and show a confidence ribbon around the curve whose width scales with 1/√n for that decade (standard-error logic); refuse to report a "fast change" or "revisit" finding for any decade pair where either side has fewer than, say, 8 dated works, and say explicitly "too few dated works in this decade to measure" rather than silently omitting or — worse — reporting a shaky number. Use a fixed, reusable embedding basis (PCA fit once across the *whole* archive, not re-fit per painter) so trajectories are comparable across painters rather than each living in its own incomparable, re-randomized 2D space.

## 6. Topological persistence: finding "the number of distinct palette families" instead of asserting it

**Pitch:** replace the single hard clustering choice in `oo_collections.py` ("cluster paintings' histograms into families") with a multi-scale topological view that shows *all* plausible numbers of families at once, honestly, instead of committing to one.

**Mechanism.** The existing family-detection step picks one clustering of a painter's paintings by Hellinger distance between their swatch-histograms. Any single clustering hides a real question: is "3 families" actually a robust count, or is it an artifact of wherever the clustering algorithm's parameter happened to land? **Persistent homology** on the Hellinger-distance point cloud (build a Vietoris–Rips filtration over increasing distance thresholds, track how many connected components — H0 — survive at each threshold) produces a **persistence diagram/barcode**: each candidate cluster count comes with a literal *lifetime* (the range of thresholds over which it exists as a distinct component). A family that's a genuine, robust mode in the painter's output shows up as a long bar; a family that's just two accidentally-close paintings shows up as a short-lived blip. This turns "how many palette families does this painter really have" from a single arbitrary number into a rigorously quantified, falsifiable claim with its own uncertainty built in — which is a mathematically much stronger and more honest object than today's single clustering.

**Data used.** The swatch-histogram-per-painting matrix already computed as an intermediate step inside `tools/oo_collections.py`'s family detection. No new data; a genuinely new algorithm layered on an existing intermediate artifact.

**User sees.** Nothing changes on the calm surface (still "3 recurring palette families" shown as swatch groups); the deeper tap-through layer gets an honest annotation when the count is shaky: "this painter's palette doesn't split cleanly into distinct groups — treat these three as a rough guide, not firm categories" for painters whose barcode has no long bars, versus confident, bar-backed language for painters (Rothko-adjacent colorists, hypothetically) whose families are topologically robust.

**Self-critique.** Persistent homology is overkill machinery for what might often resolve to "3 fuzzy blobs, no robust structure" — it's expensive to explain and risks becoming math theater that impresses engineers but tells users nothing they couldn't get from "these clusters are rough." It's also sensitive to the same sparse-data problem as #5 for painters with few paintings.

**Improved version.** Don't surface the barcode itself to users at all — use it purely as an internal *confidence gate* that decides whether the existing family UI says "3 clear families" or "a rough 3-way split" in plain English. The topology does real work (deciding which of two tones of voice to use) without ever asking a user to understand topology, which fits "depth by default, calm on the surface" far better than exposing the machinery.

## 7. Graph-theoretic "color companionship": a co-occurrence network instead of a flat lift vector

**Pitch:** the existing hue-lift vector (a painter's vivid-area share per hue bin vs. the archive) says *which* hues a painter overuses, but says nothing about which hues they pair *together* — and pairing is exactly what David's three reference palettes are about (always one dark anchor + a blue/yellow tension).

**Mechanism.** Build a weighted graph G where nodes are hue-bins (the same 12 OKLab bins already used for the lift vector) and edge weight(i,j) = co-occurrence strength of bins i and j *within the same painting*, specifically the lift of "painting contains both a vivid-i area and a vivid-j area" over the independence baseline (if painters in general paired i and j no more than chance, lift ≈ 1; a painter who structurally pairs cobalt-blue regions with ochre regions far more than chance gets a strong edge). This is already half-built: the per-painter analysis already computes "color pairs with lift" at the color level; this generalizes it to the hue-bin level and represents it as a graph rather than a flat list, which unlocks graph algorithms — **maximum-weight independent "anchor" selection** (which single hue is this painter's most load-bearing partner across all their strong pairings — i.e., graph centrality) and **community detection** (does this painter have two or three recurring hue-pairing "regimes" rather than one).

**Data used.** The existing pool/hue data already underlying the lift vector (`data/games/oo/<kind>/<id>.json`'s `lift` field) and the per-painter "color pairs with lift" in `data/analysis/artists/<slug>.json`, regridded from specific colors to the 12 hue bins for tractability. No new raw data, a new relational structure over existing numbers.

**User sees.** A small node-link diagram (or, more calmly, just the top 2–3 strongest pairings stated in prose: "van Gogh pairs cobalt blue with ochre-gold far more than chance would predict") directly licensing a *generator*: "build me a palette anchored on this painter's strongest real pairing" — which is a mechanized version of exactly what David pointed at in his three reference examples ("a complementary or near-complementary tension... chosen for character").

**Self-critique.** Co-occurrence *within a painting* conflates true compositional pairing (the painter deliberately placing these two hues together) with simple popularity (two hues that are each independently common will show inflated raw co-occurrence even with no real relationship) unless the lift normalization is done carefully against each hue's marginal frequency — get the baseline wrong and you'll "discover" fake pairings that are just two popular colors.

**Improved version.** Use pointwise mutual information (already implicitly what "lift" is, log-form) rather than raw co-occurrence, and additionally require the pairing to hold up *within single paintings* at above-baseline *simultaneous area share* (not just presence/absence) so a hue that merely appears as a 0.3% fleck next to a dominant one doesn't count as a structural pairing.

## 8. The simplex-volume "palette richness" score

**Pitch:** one clean scalar, with real geometric meaning, for "how much ground does this palette cover" — usable for leaderboards, comparisons, and badges, replacing vague "vividness" claims with an actual volume.

**Mechanism.** For any k-color palette, its colors are k points in the 3D OKLab solid. Compute the **volume of their convex hull** (for k ≥ 4; for k=3 use the triangle's area, for k < 3 it degenerates, which itself is an honest signal that "too few colors to measure spread"). This single number — literally a geometric volume, computable with `scipy.spatial.ConvexHull` (already used elsewhere in the analysis pipeline for gamut area in 2D) extended to 3D — is a rigorous stand-in for "richness" that two colors squeezed into one corner of color space can never fake, no matter how "vivid" each one looks individually in isolation.

**Data used.** Any palette already generated by #1/#2. Trivial to compute, no new data.

**Self-critique + improvement, briefly** (to keep this entry tight): raw hull volume is biased toward palettes that include one extreme outlier color, double-counting a problem #1 already needed to fix — so ship it only alongside a dispersion-penalized version, and always report it as a percentile against the archive's own distribution of hull volumes (itself trivial to precompute once, for every existing painting's pool) rather than a bare, uncalibrated number, so "this palette's volume is large" always comes with "...larger than 85% of painted palettes in the archive" — consistent with the "measure, then tell" rule, and with real statistical grounding instead of an arbitrary threshold.

## My one wild idea: the Archive as a single Riemannian color-manifold, and "style" as a metric tensor

Take every one of the ~42,000 paintings' pools and stack them into one enormous point cloud in a feature space (hue histogram × value stats × harmony fit × warm/cool balance — on the order of 30–50 dimensions). This is not a list of paintings anymore; it's a **sampled manifold** — the shape, in high-dimensional space, of "how color has actually been used across 500+ years of painting in this archive." Fit a diffusion map or UMAP embedding *once*, globally (not per-painter, which was #5's flaw), to get a shared 2–3D "map of all color-use in art." Every painting, painter, movement, decade and country becomes a located region or a density cloud on *one shared map* — and because it's one shared embedding, distances on it are finally comparable across every collection type at once: you can ask "is Hokusai's palette-region closer to Impressionism's region or to Rococo's," literally, geometrically, not as a vague essay claim.

Push it further: at each point on this manifold, estimate a local covariance (a tiny ellipsoid capturing "how much paintings right around here vary in color") — this is a **metric tensor** in the differential-geometry sense, and it lets you define geodesics: the *shortest path through actual painted history* from, say, Rembrandt's dark tenebrism to Monet's late water-lily chroma, passing only through regions the archive has real evidence for, rather than a naive straight line through RGB space that would pass through colors nobody painted. That geodesic, rendered as a sequence of real paintings nearest to each waypoint, becomes an entirely new kind of content: a browsable "color history path" between any two painters, movements, or decades, built from a provably shortest, evidence-grounded route rather than an editorial guess — genuinely something no competitor's tool (none of which model more than a single image's colors at a time) could produce, because none of them have modeled the *whole corpus* as one connected geometric object in the first place.

The honest caveat this idea demands, loudly: the manifold's shape is entirely a function of which museums digitized which painters, so "distance" on it partly measures "digitization overlap," not "true stylistic distance" — a disclosure the app would need to carry on every such path, consistent with the dataset's own documented sampling bias.
