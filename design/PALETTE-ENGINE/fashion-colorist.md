# The core insight from my field

Fashion and interior colorists never ask "what are the colors" — they ask "what is the *role* of each color, and in what *proportion*." A trend forecast isn't a list of hexes; it's a ratio (60-30-10, or a seasonal seven-color story with a dominant, a complement, and "the surprise"), a *temperature* (warm-dominant vs cool-dominant, which governs the whole mood before anyone names a hue), and a *texture/finish* layer that a flat swatch erases completely (matte gouache-navy reads as a totally different color from glossy lacquer-navy at the identical hex). We also know that beauty in applied color is almost never about the rarest hue — it's about **tension managed by proportion**: a tiny hit of a loud color against a disciplined field of quiet ones reads as "bold," while the same loud color at 50% reads as "cheap" or "aggressive." Palette tools built by engineers extract colors; they almost never extract *proportion discipline*, *temperature balance*, or *role assignment* (anchor / support / accent / spark), which is exactly the vocabulary David used instinctively when he picked his three van Gogh references — one deep anchor, a tension pair, distinct small color each playing a different job. My field also knows that palettes travel across media (a dress, a sofa, a lipstick, a room) by translating *role and proportion*, not by copying hex values — which is the single biggest thing a "derive a palette from a painting" tool is missing: it should output a *role-and-ratio recipe*, not just five chips.

# System ideas

## 1. The Role-and-Ratio Engine (not just colors — jobs and proportions)
**Pitch:** Every generated palette gets each color tagged with a *role* (Anchor, Tension, Support, Spark) and a *ratio*, the way a stylist or interior designer actually specifies a scheme — turning "5 pretty colors" into an actionable recipe.

**Mechanism:** For a painting's 24-color pool (`data/gallery/d/NNN.json`, field with r,g,b,share), don't just pick N colors — classify roles from three signals computed per color: (a) **area share** (from the pool's weight field) → candidates above ~15% share are Anchor candidates; (b) **chroma rank** relative to the painting's own chroma distribution (not raw OKLab C, but perceived saturation = C relative to the sRGB-gamut max C at that L,h, same correction already used in the vivid-pair picker in `oo_collections.py`) → the top 1-2 by chroma that are *not* the anchor become Tension/Spark candidates; (c) **hue-complement check**: among chroma-ranked candidates, find the pair whose hue angle in OKLab is closest to 150-210° apart (near-complementary, matching all three of David's references — none are exact 180° complements, they cluster near-complementary) → that pair gets tagged Tension (the "blue vs yellow/orange" job). Remaining colors by descending share fill Support; the single lowest-share, highest-chroma outlier becomes Spark. Ratios are just each role's summed area share, re-expressed as a 60-30-10-style sentence by snapping to the nearest of a small ratio vocabulary (70-20-10, 60-25-10-5, 50-30-15-5) via minimum L1 distance — never inventing a ratio the painting doesn't roughly have.

**Data used:** `data/gallery/d/NNN.json` pool (rgb+share), perceived-saturation formula already built for Odd-One-Out. Nothing missing — this is a re-labeling layer on existing extraction, no new data needed.

**User sees:** On a painting page, alongside the existing By-area/Diverse/Harmonies modes, a new "Recipe" view: four swatches labeled in small caps (Anchor · Tension · Support · Spark) with a ratio bar like a stacked progress bar, and one sentence: *"Night Café: a near-black-navy anchor (46%), cobalt pulling against lemon yellow across the room (31% combined), warm orange glow as support, a spark of green felt at 4%."* Tap any swatch → full color page. Example: on **Café Terrace at Night**, the engine should surface cobalt+lemon as the Tension pair and the near-black navy as Anchor — testing it against David's own hand-picked reference is the validation step before shipping.

**Self-critique:** Ratio-snapping can feel fake-precise ("exactly 60-30-10") when the real distribution is messy, and forcing every painting into Anchor/Tension/Support/Spark will misfire on paintings that are genuinely monochrome or have no real complementary tension (a lot of Whistler nocturnes have one hue family only) — the algorithm would hallucinate a "tension" pair that's really just noise in the chroma ranking.

**Improved version:** Add a confidence/applicability gate: only show the Tension role when the hue-angle gap of the top two chroma candidates is within a tolerance band AND both have perceived-saturation above a minimum floor (say, top quartile of the archive's saturation distribution) — otherwise the view relabels itself honestly as "Quiet palette: one dominant hue family, no complementary tension" with Anchor/Support/Spark only, which is itself an honest, teachable finding rather than a forced one.

## 2. The "Believable Not Average" Palette — a beauty/typicality objective function
**Pitch:** Directly builds David's stated want — "it's about harmony and beauty and uniqueness, not about average" — as an explicit scoring function instead of a vibe.

**Mechanism:** Define a per-painting score `Beauty(p) = w1·Tension(p) + w2·Distinctness(p) − w3·Muddiness(p)`, where: **Tension** = max perceived-saturation among the painting's top-3 pool colors by area (rewards vivid, not average, colors — directly fixing "boring dull colors, cuz that doesn't even feel like him"); **Distinctness** = the painting's percentile rank within its own painter's archive on a hue-histogram distance metric (already computed for "most typical / least typical painting" in `data/analysis/artists/<slug>.json`) — take paintings *far* from typical, i.e., in the painter's own stylistic "colorful stages" as David said about van Gogh; **Muddiness** = share of the painting's area with chroma below a floor (penalizes brown-soup palettes). Rank the painter's whole oeuvre by `Beauty(p)` and surface the top decile as "Most beautiful & unique palettes" — exactly the "palette calculator" David asked for, and it is a *ranking of real paintings*, not a synthetic blend.

**Data used:** `data/analysis/artists/<slug>.json` (clusters, extremes, decade-by-decade palette, change point), `data/gallery/d/` pool shares, perceived-saturation formula. The "change point" and "palette by decade" fields let this explicitly route to a painter's most-saturated *period* (e.g., van Gogh's Arles/Saint-Rémy years), which is exactly the "colorful stages of his life" mechanism David described.

**User sees:** A new painter-page module, "His most beautiful palettes" (David's literal phrase), a small horizontal carousel of 4-8 paintings ranked by Beauty(p), each showing its variable-count palette (see #3) and one honest line: *"From 1888-89, his most chroma-intense period — darker, muddier work from the Nuenen years scores lower on this measure, which only tracks vividness and distinctness, not quality."* Example: van Gogh's carousel should foreground Arles/Saint-Rémy works; a control room validation against his known-dull early Nuenen palette (which should score low) is the test.

**Self-critique:** "Beauty" as vividness + distinctness is a reasonable proxy but conflates *visual intensity* with *aesthetic quality* — it will systematically favor late-period, high-chroma painters and undervalue masters of restraint (Vermeer, Chardin) who are beautiful *because* they're quiet; calling this "beauty" risks the hubris the honesty rules warn against, and the word itself oversells a chroma-and-distinctness score.

**Improved version:** Rename the user-facing label to "Vivid & distinctive," reserve "beautiful" for David's own internal spec language only, and ship it per-painter rather than cross-painter (never rank Vermeer against van Gogh on this axis) — plus add a toggle so a painter whose whole oeuvre is restrained (the Muddiness term would tank everyone) gets a *relative* version: distinctness and tension computed against that painter's own distribution, never the archive's.

## 3. Variable-N Palette Extraction via a Minimum-Description-Length Elbow
**Pitch:** Directly answers "variable amount of colors, not just limited to 5" with a principled stopping rule instead of an arbitrary slider default.

**Mechanism:** The app already does farthest-point OKLab sampling for Diverse mode with fixed k (3/6/12/20). Instead, run the existing greedy farthest-point selection outward from k=2 to k=20 against the 24-color pool, and at each step compute the **marginal reconstruction gain**: ΔE(k) = mean OKLab distance from every pool color (weighted by area share) to its nearest of the k selected swatches, same error metric `oo_collections.py` already uses to size swatch books. Plot gain-per-added-color = ΔE(k-1) − ΔE(k). The "natural" palette size is the elbow: the smallest k where adding one more color yields a gain below a fixed-small threshold (same 0.02 OKLab target used for swatch-book sizing) — i.e., every real distinguishable color family in the painting is captured and no more. This gives *per-painting* variable counts driven by the painting's own complexity, not a slider David has to touch.

**Data used:** `data/gallery/d/NNN.json` pool; reuses the existing `glPoolDiverse` farthest-point algorithm and the k-selection logic from `tools/oo_collections.py`, just applied per-painting instead of per-collection.

**User sees:** Default palette view has no visible count — it just looks "right" (a sparse Vermeer interior might naturally elbow at 4; a crowded Bruegel market scene might elbow at 14). The existing 3/6/12/20 slider stays as a manual override, now labeled "Auto (recommended): N" at the top so the system's choice is visible and legible, not hidden magic.

**Self-critique:** An automatic elbow can feel inconsistent across paintings ("why does this one get 6 and that one get 11?") without explanation, and will sometimes produce an aesthetically awkward count (7 colors look worse in a UI than 6 or 8 for layout reasons alone).

**Improved version:** Snap the elbow's raw k to the nearest value in a curated "pleasant count" set {3,4,5,6,8,10,12,16,20} (never raw 7, 9, 11, 13) and always show the one-line reason — *"12 colors: this canvas has 12 real color families within a visible-difference threshold"* — so the variable count teaches the "measure, then tell" philosophy instead of looking arbitrary.

## 4. Seasonal Capsule Generator — 60/30/10 wearable translations of a painting
**Pitch:** Turns any painting's Role-and-Ratio recipe (#1) into an actual outfit or room capsule spec, the single thing no competitor (Adobe Color, Coolors, Khroma) even attempts, because none of them think in garments/rooms at all.

**Mechanism:** Map the four roles from #1 directly onto classic proportion slots: Anchor → "Base" (60%, typically a neutral-to-deep garment: coat, trouser, sofa, wall); Support → "Secondary" (30%: sweater, rug, accent wall); Tension-pair's less dominant member and Spark → combined into "Accent" (10%: shoes/bag/scarf, or a cushion/vase). Each slot additionally gets a **wearability adjustment**: desaturate Anchor and Support toward realistic textile/paint gamuts by clipping chroma to the sRGB gamut boundary already used for perceived-saturation (raw painting pigments can exceed what a dye or paint can reproduce; the museum-photograph caveat already forces honesty here), while Accent/Spark is allowed to stay at full measured chroma since accents are exactly where fashion tolerates the most saturated, impractical color. Output a second, muted "day" variant (chroma × 0.6 on Base/Secondary only) and a "night/evening" variant (chroma unchanged, but Anchor swapped for the painting's darkest measured pool color) — this is literally how seasonal forecasting services generate day/evening capsule variants from one "story" palette.

**Data used:** Role-and-Ratio output from #1 (which reuses pool rgb+share from `data/gallery/d/NNN.json`); no new data, though a genuine textile-gamut clip (vs. the sRGB-screen clip used here as a stand-in) would need real dye/pigment gamut data we don't have — flagged as an honest proxy, not a real textile match.

**User sees:** On a painting page, below Recipe (#1), a "Wear this painting" card: four color-blocked rectangles sized to 60/30/10 proportions labeled Base/Secondary/Accent, a toggle for Day/Evening, and one caveat line: *"Approximated for screen and everyday dye — not matched to a real fabric or paint gamut."* Example: **Wheatfield with Crows** → Base: oxblood red-brown (coat), Secondary: sap green (knit), Accent: warm yellow + cobalt (scarf, shoe) — matching David's own described reading of that painting almost exactly, since his reference palette already reads as a capsule.

**Self-critique:** Mapping "Anchor" to "coat/base" assumes dark, large-area colors are always wearable as a base, but some paintings' largest-share color is a pale sky or wall that would make a bizarre garment base; also "wearability" claims (skin-tone compatibility, trend-cycle currency) require data (skin-tone models, current trend-cycle calendars) the archive doesn't have at all.

**Improved version:** Gate the capsule generator behind a lightness check — only promote Anchor to "Base" if its L is in the lower half of the painting's range (true anchors read as grounding, not floating); if the largest-share color is pale/high-L, relabel the slot "Field" (wall/background color) instead of "Base," and explicitly scope out any skin-tone or trend-currency claim with a stated "not covered: how this reads on skin, or current season trend status" rather than silently guessing.

## 5. Cross-Painter "Palette Family" Matching for Personal Color / Capsule Shopping
**Pitch:** "If you love Monet's blues, here's your palette family across 5 other painters, decades and even non-painting collections (gems, flowers)" — the many-lenses-one-color philosophy applied as literal styling guidance.

**Mechanism:** Represent any palette (a painting's Role-and-Ratio set, or a user's own uploaded photo/wardrobe swatch — future) as a fixed-length feature vector: hue-bin histogram (the same 12 × 30° OKLab hue bins as the Hue Lift vector in `data/games/oo/<kind>/<id>.json`), mean L, mean perceived-saturation, and a tension-angle (the hue-gap of its top Tension pair from #1). Nearest-neighbor search (cosine or weighted Euclidean) across all painters'/movements'/decades' Hue Lift vectors already computed, *and* across the "looks" palettes (`data/looks.js`, which already span fashion eras, gems, botany, film) and the per-painting Diverse palettes stored per collection in the Odd-One-Out works array — a single embedding space that already spans painting, fashion-era, gem, and flower data without building anything new structurally, just indexing what's already computed.

**Data used:** `data/games/oo/<kind>/<id>.json` lift vectors (already built for 770 collections: painters, movements, decades), `data/looks.js` palettes (fashion eras, gems, botany already present per the repo description). Matching a user's own photo is NOT yet supported (no camera/photo-extraction pipeline exists) — would need a client-side color-extraction step (k-means on an uploaded image, feasible client-side with existing OKLab math) before it could recommend a personal capsule from a real wardrobe.

**User sees:** On any painting or painter page, "Lives in this palette family too" — a horizontal strip crossing categories: a Klimt gold-and-jewel-tone painting linking to a matching Art Deco "look," a specific gemstone, and a Sargent portrait with a similar accent structure. Tap any → its own full page (one-tap rule honored). Example: Monet's violet-heavy late Giverny work (Hue Lift already flags blue-violet as his most over-represented family) should surface alongside "lavender/wisteria" named colors and botanical entries with the same hue bin.

**Self-critique:** Cross-category matching on hue histogram alone will produce matches that are *technically* close in hue-space but feel wrong (matching a somber Rembrandt brown-black to a cheerful "autumn leaf" look just because both sit in warm-dark hue bins ignores mood/chroma-texture entirely), and risks feeling like generic "similar colors" clickbait rather than genuine insight.

**Improved version:** Require matches to agree on *both* hue histogram shape (cosine similarity above a threshold) AND the overall L/chroma "mood band" (dark-muted vs light-vivid vs mid-saturated, a coarse 3×3 grid on mean L × mean perceived-saturation) before surfacing — so matches share temperament, not just hue coordinates, and cap results to the top 3 so it reads curated, not algorithmic spam.

## 6. The Decade/Movement "Color Zeitgeist" Timeline (trend-cycle forecasting, backwards)
**Pitch:** Fashion forecasting is built on trend-cycle theory (20-year revival cycles, the pendulum between maximalism and minimalism); apply that exact lens backward across `data/analysis/groups.json`'s 67 decades to show art-historical color actually cycling, then let users see where *today's* fashion colors sit in that long cycle.

**Mechanism:** For each decade (already aggregated in `groups.json`), compute mean chroma and a warm/cool balance (the warm/cool fraction field already in per-painting analysis, aggregated). Plot these two series across the ~400+ years the corpus spans (sparse early, dense from Impressionism on — be explicit about sample-size confidence per decade, since many early decades are thin and undated works get excluded). Detect local maxima/minima in the chroma series as candidate "maximalist peaks" and "restraint troughs" — this is literally the forecasting industry's maximalism/minimalism pendulum, just measured instead of asserted. Overlay (manually curated, not derived — flag clearly as editorial) known fashion-cycle peaks for comparison, e.g., noting when a current runway-reported palette trend's chroma/warmth profile rhymes with a specific historical decade's measured profile — framed as "rhymes with," never "proves," since correlation across unrelated domains (painting photographs vs. textile dye trends) is suggestive, not causal.

**Data used:** `data/analysis/groups.json` (decade aggregates), per-painting warm/cool fraction and chroma stats from `data/analysis/paintings-NNN.json`. Missing: any actual fashion/textile trend-cycle dataset (we have none) — the "today's fashion rhymes with decade X" claim needs either a hand-curated reference table maintained editorially, or it should be dropped entirely and replaced with a purely art-internal cycle view.

**User sees:** A scroll-through timeline chart (decade on X, chroma on Y, color of each point = era's dominant hue) with a plain-English caption at each peak/trough: *"The 1870s-80s: a measured chroma peak as Impressionism broke from academic browns — caveat: sample size and dating are sparse before 1850, so early-timeline comparisons should be read loosely."* No fashion-trend overlay unless editorially maintained and clearly separated visually (different layer, toggle-able, labeled "editorial note, not derived from the art data").

**Self-critique:** Decade bins conflate wildly different regions, media conditions, and photography sources — a "1600s chroma dip" might just mean "we have fewer well-photographed non-Dutch paintings from that decade," not that painters of 1600 used less saturated color; presenting it as a clean trend line risks exactly the kind of false pattern the app's honesty rules exist to prevent.

**Improved version:** Always show a sample-size sparkline under the main chart (paintings-per-decade) and suppress or visually fade any decade below a minimum-n floor (say, <15 paintings); state explicitly in the caption that country/museum-source mix shifts across decades (more Commons/European sources in later decades) as a confound, turning the chart into an honest "what we can and can't see" piece rather than a confident trend line.

## 7. Neutral-to-Statement Ratio Score ("How loud is this room?")
**Pitch:** A single, legible number — borrowed directly from interior-design practice, where every scheme is scored by what fraction is "quiet" (neutrals/base tones) vs. "statement" (anything a client would call "a color choice") — applied to any painting, painter, or movement.

**Mechanism:** Define "neutral" per color in a painting's pool as perceived-saturation below a fixed low threshold (calibrated against the archive's own saturation distribution, e.g., bottom tercile) regardless of lightness (a near-black and a pale grey are both neutral; a mid-grey-blue is borderline and counted by fractional membership via a soft sigmoid around the threshold, avoiding a hard cliff). Sum area-share of neutral vs. statement colors → one ratio, e.g. "72% neutral / 28% statement." Aggregate the same ratio across a painter's whole oeuvre (weighted by painting, using `data/analysis/artists/<slug>.json` clusters) to produce a single "loudness" number per painter, rankable against peers — this is the single most legible "stand out tenfold" number for a fashion audience because it's exactly the metric their own field already uses daily.

**Data used:** Pool rgb+share per painting; painter-level aggregation via existing artist analysis clusters. No missing data.

**User sees:** A small horizontal meter (quiet ← → loud) on painter and movement pages with the percentage, e.g., *"Vermeer: 86% neutral — among the quietest painters in the archive; his 14% statement color is almost always concentrated in a single blue."* A comparison strip lets users slide between two painters' meters. Example: Matisse should sit near the "loud" end; Whistler near the "quiet" end — a sanity check worth running before shipping.

**Self-critique:** A single number flattens the Role-and-Ratio nuance (#1) and could feel reductive against the app's own "never reduce a thing to one palette" rule — plus the neutral/statement threshold is a somewhat arbitrary cutoff that could be gamed or feel wrong at the boundary (is a dusty rose neutral or statement?).

**Improved version:** Always present the meter as the *entry* layer with the full Role-and-Ratio recipe one tap beneath it (satisfying "depth by default, calm on the surface" rather than contradicting "never one palette"), and replace the hard threshold with the soft sigmoid already specified, displaying the number with a stated tolerance ("±a few points depending on where you draw the line between muted and neutral") so it reads as an honest estimate, not false precision.

## 8. Material/Finish Inference from Local Contrast Texture (the thing flat hex swatches can never show)
**Pitch:** The single biggest gap between a painting's "colors" and how fashion/interior people actually *use* color: finish and material completely change how a color reads, and nothing here currently distinguishes "matte" from "glossy" from "textured."

**Mechanism:** Using the ~63,000 cached full-resolution images (`research/_raw/`), compute a local-contrast texture signature per dominant color region as an offline build step: for pixels nearest (in OKLab) to each top pool color, measure local standard deviation of luminance in a small window (e.g., 5×5) relative to that region's mean — high local-contrast-to-mean ratio with small spatial scale suggests impasto/textured brushwork (reads as "matte, tactile" in fashion/material terms — think bouclé, raw silk); low local variance with the color near-flat suggests glazed/smooth passages (reads as "lacquer, satin, polished"). This is a genuinely new, pixel-level signal nothing in the current pipeline computes (current analysis is color-only, not texture).

**Data used:** `research/_raw/{commons,aic,cma,smk}/` cached images (new processing, not yet done); would need a new offline build step in Python (numpy/scipy, already available) writing results into a new `data/analysis/texture-NNN.json` or an added field in the existing per-painting analysis file. This is explicitly a "missing, here's how to get it" idea — the pixel access exists, the computation doesn't yet.

**User sees:** On the Recipe/capsule views (#1, #4), each role additionally gets a one-word material suggestion: *"Anchor (oxblood): suggest a matte or brushed finish — this red sits in the painting's most textured passage"* vs. a Vermeer glaze reading *"suggest a satin or lacquer finish."* This is a genuinely novel translation layer no competitor touches because none of them look at brushwork at all.

**Self-critique:** Local contrast texture conflates brushwork scale with canvas weave, varnish craquelure, scan/photo noise, and compression artifacts from museum photography — a heavily cracked old-master glaze could read as "textured" when it's actually aging damage, not painted texture, which is a real risk of misleading users with a confident-sounding material claim from a confound.

**Improved version:** Cross-check texture signal against painting *date* and known *technique* metadata (impasto is overwhelmingly post-1870 oil technique; egg tempera and most pre-1400 panels are inherently smoother) before surfacing a material suggestion, and phrase every output as a hedge tied to the painting itself, not a general material claim: *"this passage in this photograph shows more local contrast than most of the painting — could be brushwork, craquelure, or the scan; shown as a texture hint, not a material fact."*

## 9. Skin-Tone-Aware "Does this painting flatter you?" (personal color analysis, honestly scoped)
**Pitch:** Personal color analysis (seasonal typing — "winter," "autumn," etc.) is one of fashion colorists' most requested but most pseudo-scientific tools; ColorHub could do the *honest* version — not typing the user, but showing which of a painting's Tension/Accent colors sit near-complementary to common skin-undertone ranges, with heavy caveats.

**Mechanism:** Skin reflectance spans a well-documented, narrow band of hue (~20-50° in most hue-angle systems, generally warm) with varying L and chroma (undertone = subtle hue shift within that band, not a different hue entirely — the actual science is continuous, not four discrete "seasons"). Rather than typing the user into a season (which the honesty rules should forbid as oversimplified/pseudoscientific framing), let the user pick a skin-tone swatch from a calibrated continuous slider (L × hue-angle within the skin band), then for any painting's Accent/Tension colors, compute hue-angle distance from that point and flag which measured colors sit in a classically flattering relationship (near-complementary to the chosen undertone, a well-established color-theory mechanism, not a myth) vs. which clash (too close in hue to skin at similar L, which flattens).

**Data used:** Painting pool colors (existing). Missing entirely: any skin-tone data — this would be 100% user-input-driven (their own slider pick), never inferred from a photo (which the Prohibited-action/privacy rules and simple honesty would both argue against doing via any biometric inference) and never framed as a trained "type."

**User sees:** Opt-in only, on a capsule/recipe view: "See against your tone" → a slider (light-to-deep, cool-to-warm undertone) → the Accent/Tension swatches get a small flattering/clashing tag with the mechanism stated plainly: *"Cobalt sits near-complementary to this undertone — classically flattering. This is a general color-contrast principle, not a guarantee, and skin reads differently in every light."*

**Self-critique:** Even the "honest" version risks reinforcing the idea that seasonal/personal-color-typing is more scientific than it is, and any skin-tone feature at all carries real risk of feeling reductive or uncomfortable if implemented carelessly (reducing a person to one slider position).

**Improved version:** Make it explicitly a *color-contrast teaching tool*, not an identity tool — drop any seasonal-type language entirely, frame it as "see how this specific color relates to a tone you pick," keep it fully opt-in and ephemeral (slider resets each visit, nothing is saved as a stored "profile" unless the user's own Learner Model elsewhere already does profile storage), and bury it two taps deep so it never competes with the calm default surface.

## 10. The "Capsule Wardrobe from a Movement" — proportion-aware multi-painting aggregation
**Pitch:** Instead of one painting's capsule (#4), aggregate an entire movement's or decade's Role-and-Ratio signal into a single representative capsule — "Dress like Impressionism," "Furnish like the Dutch Golden Age" — using real statistical aggregation, not a cherry-picked single painting.

**Mechanism:** For a movement (`data/analysis/groups.json`), take the union of all member paintings' Role-tagged colors (#1) and cluster them (weighted k-means in OKLab, same method as swatch books) separately *within each role bucket* (cluster all "Anchor" colors across the movement into its top 2-3 anchor archetypes, same for Tension/Support/Spark) — this produces a movement-level capsule where each slot is itself the *most common* color in that role across hundreds of paintings, which is a genuinely different (and more defensible) object than "here's one painting's palette labeled as the movement."

**Data used:** `data/analysis/groups.json` movement membership, pool colors per member painting (role-tagged via #1). No missing data; purely an aggregation of existing fields.

**User sees:** A movement page gets "The Impressionist capsule" — four role-labeled swatch clusters, each showing its *within-role* variation (e.g., Anchor shows its 2-3 most common dark-anchor hues across the movement, not one color), with the finding stated honestly: *"Across 1,200+ Impressionist paintings in this archive, the most common Anchor color is a deep warm brown-black, used in 34% of works' darkest passages — not every Impressionist painting has one."*

**Self-critique:** Movement tags are explicitly flagged in the data notes as "sparse and partly inferred from the painter" — aggregating on shaky group membership risks compounding an uncertain label into a confidently-stated "movement capsule," which could state something false with high apparent authority.

**Improved version:** Show the member count and inference-confidence note inline wherever movement membership is partly inferred ("includes N paintings tagged by museum record, M inferred from the painter's recorded movement"), and offer the same capsule computed from decade or country groupings (whose membership is factual, not inferred) as an equally prominent alternative so users aren't funneled only through the shakiest grouping.

## 11. Trend-Cycle "Rhyme Finder": today's color news, matched to history (clearly editorial)
**Pitch:** A lightweight, honestly-labeled bridge between the archive's measured history and whatever color is actually trending in fashion/design right now (Pantone Color of the Year, a widely reported runway palette), letting users ask "has this happened before?"

**Mechanism:** Maintain a small, manually-curated, dated JSON of notable named trend colors (Pantone COTY entries, etc. — explicitly editorial content, like `data/looks.js` already is for aesthetics) with their approximate hue/L/chroma in OKLab. For any such entry, nearest-neighbor search against painter/movement/decade Hue Lift vectors (already built, see #5) to surface "this hue family was also unusually prominent in [movement/decade/painter]."

**Data used:** Hue Lift vectors (`data/games/oo/`). Missing: the trend-color reference table itself — needs manual curation and a maintenance commitment (trend colors change yearly), explicitly flagged as editorial, non-derived content exactly like `looks.js`.

**User sees:** A small, clearly-labeled "In the news" card (separated visually from measured-data views) — *"2026's reported color-of-the-year sits in the same hue family this archive measures as unusually prominent in late Monet and Fauvism — a resonance, not a claim that one caused the other."*

**Self-critique:** Easiest of all these ideas to let decay into unmaintained, stale, or embarrassingly wrong "trend" claims, and the causal-sounding framing ("fashion is channeling van Gogh") is exactly the kind of overreach the honesty rules exist to prevent.

**Improved version:** Cap it to a maximum of 1-2 entries updated on a fixed annual cadence (not a constant content expectation), require the word "resonance" or "rhyme" (never "trend," "returning," or "channeling") in every surfaced sentence, and let it expire/grey out automatically after its stated year rather than accumulating stale claims.

## 12. Proportion-Fidelity Score for "Diverse" and user-made palettes
**Pitch:** A simple honesty check that could become a quiet differentiator: score any generated or user-built palette on how faithfully its *proportions* (not just its colors) match the source painting, since competitors show colors with zero attention to how much of the canvas each one actually occupies.

**Mechanism:** For the existing Diverse/By-area/Harmonies modes, compute a Proportion-Fidelity number: Σ over selected swatches of |displayed-equal-share − true-area-share|, normalized — "By area" mode scores near-perfect by construction; "Diverse" (farthest-point, which deliberately ignores area to surface rare hues) will score low, which is *correct and should be stated*, not hidden.

**Data used:** Pool area shares (existing).

**User sees:** A tiny, optional "proportion-true" badge only on modes that earn it (By area), with Diverse/Stand-out explicitly captioned *"chosen for variety, not how much canvas each color covers"* — making an invisible design decision legible, directly serving "measure, then tell."

**Self-critique:** This is a small, almost infrastructural idea rather than a showstopper system — on its own it won't make the app feel "tenfold" different to a user.

**Improved version:** Fold it as a one-line caption under every existing palette mode (near-zero net-new surface) rather than a standalone feature — cheap, honest, and reinforces every other idea's credibility rather than competing with them for attention.

## 13. "Season-Shift" Simulator — relighting a painting's palette to a different light temperature
**Pitch:** Interior/fashion colorists know a color scheme is inseparable from its light source (north light vs. golden-hour vs. gallery halogen); let users see a painting's Role-and-Ratio recipe shifted toward a different illuminant, the same move a stylist makes when choosing a palette for "morning light bedroom" vs. "evening dinner party."

**Mechanism:** Apply a von Kries-style chromatic adaptation transform (standard, well-established color-science method, not invented) shifting each role-color from the painting's inferred native illuminant (approximate from its overall mean-L/warm-cool fraction — crude but bounded) toward a target illuminant (daylight ~6500K, warm incandescent ~2700K, overcast ~7500K), recomputed in OKLab. Purely a simulation layer on top of existing extracted colors — doesn't touch extraction, only display.

**Data used:** Existing pool/role colors; mean L and warm/cool fraction fields already in per-painting analysis as a crude native-illuminant proxy.

**User sees:** A capsule view toggle: "See this capsule in: Morning light / Golden hour / Gallery light," each shifting all four role swatches together, with a blunt caveat: *"An approximation — we don't know the actual light this was painted or photographed under."*

**Self-critique:** The "native illuminant" estimate is genuinely weak (we're inferring lighting from a photograph of a painting, several removes from any ground truth), so dressing this up as colorimetrically rigorous would overclaim precision we don't have.

**Improved version:** Reframe explicitly as a *styling preview tool*, not a color-science measurement — drop any claim about the painting's "real" light and present it as "see this recipe under different light," a demonstrably honest, purely-forward simulation rather than a reconstruction claim.

## 14. "The Accent Economy" — scarcity-weighted Spark detection across a whole collection
**Pitch:** In fashion, the rarest color in a wardrobe or season is disproportionately memorable (the one red shoe); formalize that as a cross-collection scarcity score so the app can surface genuinely surprising Spark colors a human might miss skimming thumbnails.

**Mechanism:** For every swatch in a painter's swatch book (`data/games/oo/<kind>/<id>.json` `sw` field, weight per 10,000), compute inverse-frequency weighting against the *whole archive's* aggregate swatch distribution (TF-IDF, directly borrowed from text retrieval: a color's "surprise" score = its share within this painter × log(1 / its share across all painters combined)) — high score = a color this painter uses disproportionately more than the archive baseline, exactly analogous to the Hue Lift vector already computed but at the individual-swatch level rather than hue-bin level, giving finer resolution than the existing 12-bin Hue Lift.

**Data used:** `data/games/oo/<kind>/<id>.json` swatch books (already built for 770 collections) plus a new archive-wide aggregate swatch distribution (computable from the same inputs, not yet built as a single global index).

**User sees:** Under a painter's existing Hue Lift-driven findings, a more specific sentence naming an actual swatch, not just a hue family: *"[Painter]'s single most surprising recurring color, relative to every painter in this archive"* — with the swatch itself shown, becoming a genuinely new finding sentence generator feeding the existing findings UI.

**Self-critique:** TF-IDF on color swatches will be noisy at the tails — a painter with very few paintings could show a "surprising" color that's really just one outlier painting's quirk, not a genuine signature, and the archive-wide baseline itself is a sample (42,331 paintings, not all of art), so "surprising relative to the archive" always secretly means "relative to this sample."

**Improved version:** Require a minimum swatch frequency (the color must recur across multiple distinct paintings by that painter, using the "nMembers" already tracked per family, not just overall share) before calling something a signature, and always phrase it as "across this archive" explicitly in the generated sentence, never "in all of art."

# My one wild idea

**A living "Color Atelier"**: a generative system, trained entirely on this archive's own measured data (never scraped stock-photo palette tools), that doesn't just extract palettes from existing paintings but *designs new seasonal capsules by blending painters* using the Role-and-Ratio grammar (#1) as its compositional rule-set. Concretely: pick two or three painters (say, Klimt + Hokusai), extract each one's statistically-derived role archetypes (Anchor hue-cluster, Tension-pair angle-and-chroma profile, Support palette, Spark outliers — all from real measured data, #1 and #10), then generate a *new* capsule by recombination: take Klimt's anchor-darkness-and-gold-tension structure but constrained to Hokusai's actual measured hue family (indigo/Prussian-blue-adjacent blues very well attested in his real swatch book) — producing a genuinely novel palette that is honestly described as "a new combination built from two measured color grammars," never as "this is what either painter actually used." This is the fashion-forecasting industry's actual highest craft — not copying a reference, but extracting its *grammar* (proportion logic, tension logic, role logic) and recomposing it for a new context/season — applied for the first time, to my knowledge, with real statistically-derived "grammars" instead of a stylist's intuition. Shown as a seasonal capsule generator: "This season's capsule, built from [Painter A]'s structure and [Painter B]'s palette" — fully generative, fully attributed, fully honest about being a new synthesis rather than a historical fact, and because it's built on 1,472 real painter profiles, movements, and decades, the combinatorial space is enormous and genuinely unavailable anywhere else.
