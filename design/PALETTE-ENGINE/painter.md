# The Painter's Palette: What a Studio Table Knows That a Swatch Grid Doesn't

## The core insight from my field

A painter's palette is never the painting's colors — it is the small set of pigments on the table that *generate* those colors through mixture, and the record of that mixing (which colors a painter actually squeezed out, how they cut them with white or earth, which ones never touched each other, which ones got glazed thin over a dry layer) carries information that pure pixel-sampling throws away. Every palette tool in the world, including ColorHub's current "By area / Diverse / Stand out / Harmonies," reads the *canvas* — the finished mixtures sitting in the paint film — and tries to reverse-engineer beauty from them by clustering or diversity-maximizing. A painter reads the canvas differently: as the visible trace of a much smaller set of *decisions* — a mother color that tints everything, a value structure built before a single hue was chosen, a deliberate temperature contrast, pigments picked for how they physically behave (transparent vs. opaque, staining vs. non-staining) as much as for their hue. Monet's "boring dull colors" that David doesn't recognize as Monet are very often his correct, accurate base-tone mixtures — the grey-violet shadow, the broken ochre underpainting — doing their *job*, which is to make the three or four saturated notes above them look like fire. A limited palette is not a compromise; it's a compositional strategy, and the thing that makes a master's palette "unique" and "beautiful" is almost never raw chroma — it's the *relationship* between a small number of anchor colors and everything built from them. No existing competitor models relationship, hierarchy, or the physical act of mixing at all; they all model population statistics of final pixels. That's the gap.

## System ideas

### 1. The Working Palette Reconstructor
**Pitch:** Don't extract the painting's colors — extract the *paints that made them*.

**Mechanism:** Treat the painting's full pixel set (or, lacking pixels, its 24-color pool) as a convex mixture space and try to find a small basis of "primary mixtures" (6-10 colors) such that most of the painting's observed colors fall near the convex hull spanned by pairwise linear blends of that basis plus white and a dark neutral. Concretely: run non-negative matrix factorization or a constrained k-means-plus-blend search where candidate basis sets are scored by how well `observed_color ≈ Σ wᵢ·basisᵢ` (in a mixing-appropriate space — not OKLab, which is perceptual, but something closer to Kubelka-Munk pigment-mixing space, or at minimum linear sRGB, since paint mixtures are physically closer to subtractive/absorption blends than to perceptual-difference spaces) reconstructs the full color pool with low residual, weighted by area share. The basis set that minimizes reconstruction error with the fewest terms *is* the plausible "palette on the table." Cross-reference candidate pigments against the period's `PIGMENT_SINCE` hedge (already in `tools/paintings.py`) to filter out anachronistic guesses and to label candidates with real historical pigment names (lead white, yellow ochre, vermilion, Prussian blue, viridian) only when the painter's dates make them plausible — never asserted as the literal paint, always framed as "a palette of pigments like these could have mixed to approximate this canvas."
**Data used:** The 24-color pool with area shares (`data/gallery/d/NNN.json`), painter birth/death and movement (`data/artists/meta.json`) for period-plausible pigment filtering. Missing: no actual pigment-identification data (XRF/spectroscopy) exists in this corpus — this must always be framed as "a plausible mixing palette consistent with the period," never a claim about the actual paint used.
**User sees:** On a painting page, a new depth layer beneath the existing palette modes: "How this might have been mixed" — 5-8 swatches labelled with historical pigment names and a small diagram showing which observed colors in the painting are closest to blends of which two basis swatches, with a slider walking from "simple (fewest pigments)" to "complex (closer fit)." Example: a Vermeer interior might reconstruct to lead white + yellow ochre + a black + ultramarine + a red earth — five "paints on the table" that, blended, explain most of the canvas, with the famous blue drapery flagged as "straight from the tube, barely mixed" (low blend-distance from the pure basis color) versus the flesh tones flagged as "built from four of these five."
**Self-critique:** This is seductive pseudo-science risk #1 — it will *look* like a real conservator's pigment analysis to a casual user, and reconstruction error is agnostic to whether the "basis" is physically real paint or just a convenient mathematical decomposition; many different bases reconstruct equally well (NMF is non-unique), so the system can quietly present one arbitrary solution as if it were historical fact. It's also vulnerable to varnish/yellowing: an aged glaze reads as a "mixing component" that was never on the palette at all.
**Improved version:** Always show reconstruction confidence (residual error) and *always* surface at least two decompositions side by side when the top two solutions are close in error, framed explicitly as "there are multiple mixing stories that fit the colors we measured" — turning the non-uniqueness from a bug into the honest finding itself. Label clearly: "a plausible palette, not the painter's actual pigments" every time, and suppress the feature below some error threshold (if even the best basis set reconstructs badly, say so and don't show a feature at all) rather than force a bad fit onto, say, a painting with heavy craquelure or extreme yellowing.

### 2. Mother Color Detector
**Pitch:** Find the one tint that's secretly mixed into everything — the "mother color" old masters used to unify a canvas.

**Mechanism:** For each painting, compute the OKLab hue/chroma of every pool color, weighted by area share, and test whether a disproportionate number of the painting's colors sit close (within some ΔE in OKLab, say ≤6-8) to a line through the color space anchored at one specific hue — i.e., whether there's a common "cast" shared across unrelated regions (sky, skin, drapery) that wouldn't be expected if those regions were independently and purely mixed. Formally: project all area-weighted colors onto chroma-hue space, find candidate "mother hues" as local hue-histogram peaks, then for each candidate measure what fraction of the *painting's total area* lies within a tolerance band of a line from neutral grey through that hue (a "tint line") versus a baseline expectation from a hue-shuffled null (shuffle each color's hue while preserving L and C, many trials, see how much tint-line coincidence you'd expect by chance). A real mother color shows significantly more mass near its tint line than the shuffled null.
**Data used:** 24-color pool + area shares. No missing data — fully computable from what exists.
**User sees:** A single sentence and a small visual: a faint colored veil overlaid on a thumbnail of the painting ("this canvas is unified by a warm ochre undertone — look for it in the shadows, the sky, even the white collar"), tappable into "which regions carry it most" (ranked by pool-color closeness to the tint line, weighted by area). Example: a Rembrandt portrait's whole shadow structure sharing one warm umber cast that even shows up faintly diluted in the supposedly-neutral background.
**Self-critique:** Aged varnish is *itself* a mother color — a yellow-brown glaze over the whole painting's surface will manufacture a fake "mother tint" finding that is really just 300 years of oxidized linseed oil, and the system has no way to distinguish "the painter's unifying glaze" from "the varnish restorers haven't cleaned yet." This is the single biggest risk of misleading a user into thinking they're seeing the painter's hand when they're seeing museum conservation history.
**Improved version:** Cross-check the candidate mother hue against the *painter's* typical varnish-cast direction (computable once, per painter, as the modal "tint line" across their *whole* catalogue in `data/analysis/artists/<slug>.json`) — if nearly every painting by that painter shares the same cast regardless of subject or period, flag it as "likely aging/varnish, shown across the board" rather than attribute it as a conscious choice; only surface a mother-color finding as painterly intent when it's distinctive *for that specific painting relative to that painter's own baseline cast* (a lift measure, exactly like the signature-color lift the painter-analysis files already compute) — i.e., "more unified by warm ochre than his other work," not "unified by warm ochre" full stop. Always append the honest caveat line once per surfaced finding.

### 3. Value-Structure-First Palette Extraction (the "squint test" engine)
**Pitch:** Separate what a painting's *value drawing* is doing from what its *color* is doing — because painters build the first and only then layer the second.

**Mechanism:** Convert every pool color to L* and bin by share into a value histogram (already partly computed per `research/ANALYSIS.md`'s "value key high/low"), then compute a **value-chroma decoupling score**: for each value band (shadow, 0-35; mid, 35-65; light, 65-100), compute the share-weighted mean chroma and hue spread. A high-key painting that is also high-chroma across all three bands reads as "flat, poster-like" in value-structure terms even if colorful; a painting with strong value range (wide spread across shadow/mid/light) but *concentrated, consistent* hue per band reads as a classically built value drawing with color laid over it — closer to chiaroscuro technique. Score each painting on a "value-led vs. color-led" axis: (variance of L* across the full palette, share-weighted) vs. (variance of hue within each value band) — a value-led painting has high L* variance and low within-band hue variance; a color-led (e.g., Fauvist) painting inverts that.
**Data used:** Pool colors + shares; the lightness histogram and contrast-range fields already in `data/analysis/paintings-NNN.json`.
**User sees:** A toggle on the painting page — "see it in black and white" (a desaturated render, trivial to produce client-side from the same pool) paired with a one-line finding: "92% of this painting's value range comes from just three tones — the color is almost entirely decoration on top of a simple light/dark structure," or the opposite, "there's almost no value structure here at all — this painting is built from color contrast, not light and dark" (a genuinely useful, teachable distinction — Caravaggio vs. Matisse, made legible as a number instead of an art-history lecture). This naturally generates *painter-level* findings too: "Rembrandt's paintings are value-led in 94% of cases; Matisse's almost never are" — a real, computable, interesting cross-painter claim using the existing painter corpus.
**Self-critique:** The 24-color pool already discards spatial adjacency — this measure can't tell "value structure used for modeling a form" from "value structure that's just a dark corner and a bright window," i.e. it's measuring global value *variance*, not value *composition* (the thing a painter actually means by value structure — where the darks and lights sit relative to the subject). Calling it "value structure" over-claims what a palette-level (non-spatial) measure can show.
**Improved version:** Rename the surfaced claim to be honest about its level — "tonal range" and "color-per-tone consistency," not "value structure" — and gate the richer, truer version of this idea (actual spatial value mapping — luminance masks, form-modeling detection) behind the pixel-level build step described in idea 7, clearly marking the pool-only version as the lightweight proxy it is.

### 4. The Beauty-and-Uniqueness Palette Calculator (direct answer to David's ask)
**Pitch:** A palette ranked not by representativeness but by *harmonic tension and distinctiveness* — exactly the three reference palettes David likes, formalized.

**Mechanism:** David's three liked palettes share a structure: one dark/deep anchor (low L, often desaturated or near-neutral), 3-4 further colors spanning a complementary or near-complementary hue split (blue-family vs. yellow/orange-family), and *every* color perceptibly distinct from every other (pairwise ΔE2000 above some floor, say ≥20). Build a scoring function over candidate subsets of a painting's (or painter's, via the swatch book) full color set — size k variable, 3 to 20 per David's spec — that rewards: (a) **hue-circle spread** — variance of hue angle weighted toward bimodality (two clusters roughly opposite each other beats colors bunched in one arc) rather than uniform spread, scored e.g. via the resultant length of the circular mean of hue angles weighted by chroma (a *low* resultant length = well-opposed hues = good); (b) **presence of one low-L, low/mid-C anchor** — require at least one candidate with L* < 35 contributing nontrivial area or presence; (c) **pairwise distinctiveness** — minimum pairwise ΔE2000 across the set, maximized (this is the existing "Diverse" farthest-point logic, but as one term, not the whole objective); (d) **uniqueness vs. the rest of the archive** — weight candidate colors by how *infrequent* that hue-chroma-lightness combination is across the full 42,331-painting pool (a simple inverse-frequency lookup against a global hue-chroma-lightness histogram built once at pipeline time), so a vivid cobalt scores higher than an equally-vivid-but-common ochre. Optimize via a greedy/local-search selection (start with the two most hue-opposed high-chroma candidates + the best anchor, then iteratively add the candidate maximizing marginal objective gain) rather than exhaustive search, since k is small. This turns "most beautiful, most unique" into: *maximize hue opposition + ensure one deep anchor + maximize mutual distinctiveness + reward archive-rarity*, which is a genuine, inspectable objective function, not a vibe.
**Data used:** Pool colors + shares per painting (for painting-level), swatch books per painter/movement/decade (`data/games/oo/`, already computed with area-weighted OKLab k-means) for collection-level "most beautiful palette of this painter's whole career," plus a global hue-chroma-lightness frequency table computed once across all `data/gallery/d/*.json` pools (new, but trivial to build — it's a histogram over existing data, no new extraction needed).
**User sees:** A new palette mode next to "By area / Diverse / Stand out / Harmonies" — call it "Striking" or "Signature" — with the existing 3/6/12/20 slider (already variable-count in `js/gallery.js`), and at the painter level, a new screen: "Van Gogh's most beautiful palettes" showing the 3-5 individual paintings whose own Striking-score is highest, explicitly surfacing (per David's own example) that they cluster in his most colorful period — which becomes a real, checkable finding ("4 of his top 5 striking palettes are from his final two years in Provence and Auvers") rather than an assertion.
**Self-critique:** "Beautiful" is being operationalized as "high contrast + rare + anchored," which will systematically prefer loud, saturated, high-drama paintings and will *never* surface a genuinely beautiful quiet, close-valued, nearly-monochrome painting (a Whistler nocturne, a Vermeer) — the metric has a built-in taste bias toward Van-Gogh-at-his-most-Van-Gogh and against subtlety, exactly the "boring dull colors" problem David named, just inverted into always preferring the opposite extreme instead of being neutral.
**Improved version:** Ship two objective functions, not one, both using the same mechanism with different weights — "Striking" (as above, rewarding contrast/rarity/anchor) and "Harmonious" (rewarding *low* pairwise ΔE combined with hue-adjacency rather than opposition, plus a tight, narrow chroma band) — and let the painting/painter page show both side by side with one line each ("Striking: what makes this canvas pop. Harmonious: what makes it feel unified."), so the tool doesn't silently decide that drama is the only kind of beauty.

### 5. Broken Color / Optical Mixture Index
**Pitch:** Quantify how much a painter relies on *your eye* to do the mixing (Impressionist/Pointillist broken color) versus mixing on the palette before it hits the canvas.

**Mechanism:** This genuinely needs pixel data the pool can't give — adjacent, small, distinct-hue strokes sitting next to each other unmixed is a *spatial* phenomenon invisible to a 24-color area-share pool. Using the locally cached full-resolution images (`research/_raw/.../`), as an offline build step: downsample to a working resolution, run superpixel segmentation (SLIC) at a stroke-plausible scale (tuned per painting's physical size/DPI if known, else a fixed small scale), then for each superpixel compute its mean color and compare it against its immediate neighbors' mean colors in OKLab; score "opticality" as the share-weighted fraction of adjacent small-region pairs with hue difference above some threshold but similar lightness (the classic broken-color signature — two different hues juxtaposed at the same value, so they don't read as a value edge, only a color one) versus the alternative (adjacent regions blended smoothly, low local hue variance — indicating the mixing happened on the palette, not on the retina).
**Data used:** Full-resolution cached images (new processing — not yet extracted into any existing JSON; this is a genuinely missing dataset that would need a new offline build, `research/` as the working area, output committed as new `data/analysis/` fields per painting, same shard pattern as the existing analysis files).
**User sees:** A single score/badge on technique-relevant paintings: "high optical mixing — look close, these aren't blended" with a tap-to-zoom into one flagged high-contrast-stroke region of the actual image (already have the image URL per painting). Example: a Seurat or late Monet scoring very high; a Vermeer or Ingres scoring near zero.
**Self-critique:** Stroke scale varies wildly by painting size and photo resolution — a tiny oil sketch photographed at low res will falsely read as "smooth" simply because individual brushstrokes are below the pixel resolution of the source photo, confusing "painted smoothly" with "photographed too small to see the strokes." This is a sampling-bias trap specific to a mixed-resolution, multi-museum corpus.
**Improved version:** Normalize superpixel scale to the painting's *actual physical size* when known (museum metadata sometimes carries dimensions; when missing, explicitly mark the score as "resolution-limited, treat as a floor not a true reading" rather than hide the uncertainty), and only surface the badge above a minimum source-image resolution threshold — silence, not a bad number, when the photo can't support the claim.

### 6. Temperature-Interaction (Albers) Contrast Map
**Pitch:** Score how hard a painting is "pushing" warm against cool at the *same* lightness — the Albers interaction-of-color move that makes flat color feel like it's vibrating or receding.

**Mechanism:** For each pair of pool colors with similar L* (within, say, 10 units) and meaningful combined area share, compute their hue-temperature distance (warm = hue angle roughly 0-90°/330-360° in OKLab-ish terms; cool = roughly 150-270°) and their chroma product. Define a per-painting "simultaneous contrast index" as the share-weighted sum over same-lightness pairs of (temperature opposition × combined chroma), normalized. This directly operationalizes the Albers principle that warm/cool tension reads strongest when value is held constant (so the eye can't resolve the difference as "just a light/dark edge" and instead feels it as push/pull).
**Data used:** Pool colors + shares — fully available, no missing data.
**User sees:** One finding sentence plus a paired-swatch visual showing the two or three strongest same-value warm/cool pairs pulled directly from the painting: "this canvas holds its orange and blue at almost the same darkness — that's what makes them vibrate instead of just sitting next to each other." Example: Van Gogh's Café Terrace (one of David's three reference paintings) — cobalt and orange at matched lightness is exactly this phenomenon, and the system would find it without being told to look for that painting specifically.
**Self-critique:** The pool's colors are already aggregated per hue-family "seats" (per the pool's own design, keeping a seat for every present hue family) — this risks rewarding paintings that happen to have been clustered into neat matched-L pairs as a side effect of the extraction algorithm's bucket choices, not because the painter intentionally matched value. A coincidental value-match in the pool's 24-color summary isn't evidence of painterly intent.
**Improved version:** Require the matched-L pair to also have meaningful *combined* area share (not two tiny accent seats that happen to tie on L* by coincidence) before it counts, and report the score as a percentile against the painter's and archive's own distribution of this same index (reusing the existing percentile machinery in `data/analysis/paintings-NNN.json`) rather than a raw number — so "high temperature contrast" always means "higher than it usually is," which is both more honest and more interesting.

### 7. The Palette Timeline: Mixing Complexity Over a Career
**Pitch:** Track not just *which* colors a painter used by decade (already planned per `palette by decade` in the painter-analysis files) but *how many pigments it would take to mix them* — a complexity-of-palette timeline.

**Mechanism:** Run the Working Palette Reconstructor (idea 1) per decade-bucket of a painter's work instead of per painting (using the decade sub-groupings already in `data/analysis/artists/<slug>.json`'s palette-by-decade structure, pooling all paintings in that bucket), and plot the minimum basis size needed to reconstruct that decade's color population within a fixed error tolerance, across the painter's life. A rising or falling "palette complexity" curve becomes a real, falsifiable claim about an artist simplifying or complicating their working method over time.
**Data used:** Painter-analysis decade buckets (`data/analysis/artists/<slug>.json`), feeding the same reconstruction mechanism as idea 1.
**User sees:** On a painter page, a simple line chart (sparkline-scale, not a full dashboard) — "pigments needed to mix his palette, by decade" — tappable into the per-decade reconstructed basis. Example: a painter whose late work needs *fewer* basis colors despite using *more visually distinct* hues (because he's pushing colors straight from a wider set of tubes rather than mixing extensively) versus one who needs *more* basis colors in a seemingly "simple" late palette (because he's mixing harder to get subtle neutrals).
**Self-critique:** Inherits every weakness of idea 1 (non-uniqueness, varnish contamination) and compounds it with the decade-binning problem described in `research/ANALYSIS.md` (many paintings are undated, decades are uneven in sample size), so a complexity "drop" could just be an undersampled decade with too few paintings to need a large basis, not a real change in method.
**Improved version:** Suppress or grey out any decade bucket below a minimum painting count (consistent with the painter-page's existing ≥10-work percentile floor), and show sample size directly on the chart (a thin bar under each point, dot size by n) so a thin decade visibly reads as uncertain rather than confidently wrong.

### 8. Glaze vs. Opaque Reading (the "where is this thin, where is this thick" guess)
**Pitch:** Distinguish colors that look like they're sitting *over* another color (transparent glazes, typical of shadow passages and deep reds/greens in oil painting history) from colors applied solidly.

**Mechanism:** A glazed color, physically, is darker and more saturated than an opaque application of the "same" pigment at the same lightness would be, because glazing is a thin, light-absorbing film over a reflective ground rather than an opaque, light-scattering layer — glazes tend to sit at the darker, higher-chroma end of a given hue's range within a painting. Flag pool colors that are simultaneously low-L *and* unusually high-C for their hue within that specific painting (outliers from the painting's own L-vs-C trend line for that hue family) as glaze-candidates; flag colors that are mid-L and lower-C as more likely straight/opaque applications.
**Data used:** Pool colors + shares, computed per painting.
**User sees:** A technique note on paintings from periods/movements where glazing was common (cross-reference movement tag): "the deep reds in the shadows here sit exactly where a thin glaze would — darker and more intense than a solid mix of the same hue would usually be."
**Self-critique:** This is a plausible-sounding but genuinely unverifiable physical inference from color statistics alone — there is no actual surface/gloss/texture information in the pool (or even in the cached photographs, reliably, given inconsistent museum lighting), so this is the single most speculative idea in this list and risks the "fabricated result" line most directly.
**Improved version:** Downgrade from a claim to a *question*: phrase it as "this could be a thin glaze — paintings don't tell us their layer structure from a photograph, but this is the pattern glazing usually leaves," and gate it to only appear on movements/periods where documented glazing technique is well attested (Flemish/Dutch Golden Age, academic Salon painting), never offered as a generic feature.

### 9. Chiaroscuro Score / Dramatic-Lighting Index
**Pitch:** A single honest number for "how much this painting relies on extreme light/dark drama" — distinct from the value-structure idea because it specifically measures *range and concentration at the extremes*, not overall distribution shape.

**Mechanism:** From the existing L* histogram per painting, compute the share-weighted 5th and 95th percentile of L* (already planned — "contrast range p5-p95" is listed in ANALYSIS.md) and additionally compute what fraction of total area sits in the extreme bottom decile (L*<10) and extreme top decile (L*>90) simultaneously — a painting with large *simultaneous* mass at both extremes and little in the middle is chiaroscuro; a painting with a wide p5-p95 range but gradual, even distribution across it is just "high key-range," a different thing.
**Data used:** Fully covered by existing/planned lightness histogram fields in `data/analysis/paintings-NNN.json`.
**User sees:** A badge/finding "dramatic lighting — nearly a third of this canvas is in true shadow, almost none in between" with cross-painter ranking ("more extreme than 95% of Caravaggio's own work," using the painter percentile machinery already planned).
**Self-critique:** Minor and easily fixed — the main risk is just double-counting with idea 3's value-led/color-led axis and confusing users with two overlapping "value" findings on one page.
**Improved version:** Merge presentation with idea 3 under one "Light & Shadow" depth layer rather than shipping as a separate competing finding — same underlying computation, one coherent narrative slot on the page instead of two redundant ones.

### 10. Cross-Painter "If you mixed like X" Palette Transfer
**Pitch:** Take any painting (or photo, eventually) and show what its colors would become if re-mixed through a specific painter's characteristic palette logic — not just "colors like Monet" but "this scene, run through Monet's actual signature-color-and-pair statistics."

**Mechanism:** Use the painter-analysis signature-color/avoided-color lift data and color-pair lift data (already computed per painter) to build a per-painter "transfer function": for a query image's extracted palette, map each query color to the nearest-hue-family bucket, then substitute that bucket's share with the target painter's own typical hue/chroma/lightness statistics *for that hue family and that role* (accent/light/shadow — the within-painting role-color lift data already exists too), preserving the query's area-share structure (its "shape") but recoloring it with the target painter's statistical fingerprint.
**Data used:** Signature/avoided colors with lift, role-colors with lift, color-pair lift (`data/analysis/artists/<slug>.json`) — all exists. Missing: applying this to an arbitrary user photo (not in the corpus) needs a client-side or build-time palette extractor for uploaded images, which doesn't currently exist in the app per the files described.
**User sees:** On a painter page: "see a sunset through Monet's eyes" — pick any of the app's own existing palette sources (another painter's painting, a flower, a gem) and recolor its swatch structure through this painter's statistical fingerprint, with a clear "a statistical impression, not a real Monet" disclaimer.
**Self-critique:** This is the idea most likely to feel like a gimmick/toy rather than a serious insight tool, and "statistical fingerprint" recoloring can produce results that are technically correct but visually nonsensical (wrong role mapping putting a "shadow-statistics" color where the original image's brightest highlight was).
**Improved version:** Constrain substitution to preserve the query's own lightness ordering strictly (only hue/chroma shift, lightness anchored to the original structure) so the result stays recognizable as "the same picture, different colorist," and frame it explicitly as a teaching tool for the lift statistics themselves ("this is what 'Monet avoids true black 4x less than his peers' looks like applied to a new image") rather than as a standalone generator.

## My one wild idea: The Palette Genome — a mixing-space embedding of the entire corpus

Take every painting's Working-Palette-Reconstructor basis (idea 1) across all 42,331 paintings and embed them in a shared low-dimensional "mixing space" — not a color space, a *palette-structure* space, where the axes are things like basis size, hue-opposition of the basis, anchor depth, chroma ceiling, and glaze-likelihood (idea 8) — essentially a fingerprint of *how* a painting was built, independent of *what color* it happens to be. Run UMAP or t-SNE over this space across the whole corpus. The result is a map where a Vermeer and a Rembrandt sit close together not because they share hues (they may not) but because they share a *mixing logic* — small basis, deep anchor, glazed shadows — and a Fauvist painting sits in a completely different neighborhood from an Impressionist one even if their raw hue palettes overlap, because their *construction* differs. This map would let a user discover "paintings built like this one" as a genuinely new relation — orthogonal to "paintings that look similar" (already covered by nearest-neighbour-by-color) and to "paintings by the same movement" (a label, not a measurement). It would let David's "stand out tenfold" claim become literally true: no competitor has ever tried to cluster art by *mixing method* rather than by resulting color or by art-historical label, because no competitor has pixel access to 63,000 full-resolution images and a reason to reconstruct mixing structure from them. The failure mode is obvious and worth naming up front: the whole tower rests on idea 1's reconstruction, which is non-unique and arguable — so this map must always be presented as "paintings that share a plausible mixing structure," one more inferential layer removed from ground truth, never as a settled taxonomy, and probably needs its axes validated against a handful of paintings with genuinely documented technique (conservators' technical literature exists for famous works) before it's trusted even provisionally.
