Sepia or Taupe?

Warmer and browner than taupe.

Compare: Taupe

Originally ink from the cuttlefish, whose Latin name is sepia.

History

Sepia is cuttlefish ink: sepia is the Greek and Latin word for cuttlefish. It was a writing ink in Greco-Roman times, and in the late 1700s a Dresden professor, Jakob Seydelmann, refined it into a concentrated paint for artists.

Science

Old brown photographs owe nothing to cuttlefish. Sepia toning converts the silver of a black-and-white print into silver sulfide, which stands up to air pollution far better, so toned prints last longer. The warm brown was a side effect of preservation.

Kin

  • Silver: Sepia photos are silver images turned to silver sulfide
  • Umber: Painters' brown from the earth, as sepia was brown from the sea
  • Charcoal: Two classic drawing materials that named colors
