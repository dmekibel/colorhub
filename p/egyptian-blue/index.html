Egyptian blue

The first color people made from scratch, over 5,000 years ago. Under infrared light it still glows.

Made from
Sand, lime, copper and alkali, fired
Chemistry
Calcium copper silicate (CaCuSi4O10)
Party trick
Glows in near-infrared

Egyptian blue is the oldest known synthetic pigment: not dug up but cooked. Heat sand, lime, a copper source (ore or bronze scrap) and a little alkali to around 850–950 °C, and you get blue crystals of calcium copper silicate. The earliest known example, on a bowl from Hierakonpolis now in Boston's Museum of Fine Arts, dates to around 3250 BCE.

Egyptians painted tombs, statues and coffins with it for three thousand years, and the Romans called it caeruleum, probably from caelum, the sky, a root shared by our word cerulean. The architect Vitruvius gave a recipe in the 1st century BCE, and Roman sources say a man named Vestorius brought production from Alexandria to Pozzuoli near Naples. After Rome the know-how faded, and the pigment dropped out of use until chemists reconstructed it in the 1800s.

Its strangest property was noticed only recently. Egyptian blue absorbs visible light and gives off strong, long-lasting infrared light, just past the red end of the spectrum: invisible to us, bright to an infrared camera. Conservators use it to find faded traces of blue on statues and walls that look unpainted, and it has turned up in places no one expected, including Raphael's 16th-century fresco The Triumph of Galatea.

It also corrects a myth. People in the ancient world made and traded a bright, stable blue, whatever their word lists say: Theophrastus called it kyanos. When Homer skips blue, it's a matter of vocabulary, not eyesight. Its modern cousins include cobalt blue, Prussian blue, synthetic ultramarine and the 2009 newcomer YInMn blue.
