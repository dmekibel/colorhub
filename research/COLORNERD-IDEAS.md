# Color Nerd: ideas for ColorHub

Research pass over the YouTube channel [Color Nerd (@ColorNerd1)](https://www.youtube.com/@ColorNerd1/videos), done 2026-10-07.
Everything here is paraphrased. No transcripts live in this file (raw notes stay in the gitignored `research/_raw/colornerd/`). Quotes are capped at 15 words.

**Method:** the video list came from the channel's own page data (videos, Shorts and live tabs). 20 videos were transcribed with tactiq.io's free transcript tool in Claude in Chrome (YouTube auto-captions, so some names come through garbled; those are flagged). Per-video notes are in the appendix at the end.

**The one-paragraph takeaway:** the channel's core message is that color is a 3D space (hue, lightness, chroma), not a wheel; that most classroom rules (RYB primaries, painter's complements, warm/cool halves, hue-angle harmony) are history, not science; and that *names live in regions of that space*. That last point is gold for ColorHub: our neighbor lines ("greener and darker than turquoise") are the same device Webster's Third used in 1961, and the channel hands us a dozen drills, labs and stories that make the 3D idea playable on a phone.

## 1. Who and what the channel is

- **Color Nerd** is a working art teacher (they mention their own classroom, art classes and color workshops) who explains color science for hobbyist artists and designers. The channel's own pitch: color theory that is neither too shallow nor too academic, for artists "tired of advice that doesn't hold up."
- About 41K subscribers and 112 uploads (71 long-form videos, 36 Shorts, 5 live streams) as of 2026-10-07. Active since about 2022.
- The current flagship series is **Color Wheel Wednesday** (weekly since about April 2026, 28+ episodes): each episode takes one historical or unusual color wheel (Ostwald, Schaeffer, Gerritsen, PCCS, LEGO, Kolormondo, quilters' wheels) and tests it against modern color science.
- Running stance: the red-yellow-blue 12-hue wheel is dogma; color is three-dimensional (hue, lightness, chroma); harmony is about paths and planes through that 3D space; warm/cool is spectral bias, not a fixed property. The creator builds their own "science-based" color disk and pigment charts and sells a pigment chart on Gumroad.
- Biggest hits are Shorts: "RYB vs. CMY Color Wheels" (3.5M views), the "Olo" new-color short (340K), "is Green WARM or COOL?" (114K). Biggest long-form: the Japan/PCCS episode (157K) and the Kolormondo globe episode (101K).

## 2. The most valuable ideas and facts

Paraphrased. Timestamps are approximate (auto-caption chunks). ⚠ marks a claim to verify before it goes into the app.

**Color is a space, and names are regions of it**
1. **The wheel is one slice of a 3D solid.** Every hue reaches its most vivid point at a different lightness: yellow and lime near the top, magenta mid-way, indigo/blue-violet low. Some hues also reach further out in chroma (magenta about 12 Munsell steps, blue about 8). So each "hue plane" has its own shape. ([Gd1qsQgsE5w @2:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Gd1qsQgsE5w&t=154s), [lXDCQjeM8Hk @0:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lXDCQjeM8Hk))
2. **Hue planes are how naming works.** Variations of one hue get named by region: pale, vivid, muted, dark; or the ISCC-NBS modifiers (pale, light, brilliant, strong, vivid, moderate, grayish, dark, deep); or Japan's PCCS tones (soft, dull, grayish, light grayish…). ([Gd1qsQgsE5w @3:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Gd1qsQgsE5w&t=186s))
3. **What people actually call colors.** In Dimitris Mylonas's online naming survey, people say *lime* rather than yellow-green or chartreuse, *purple* rather than violet, and use *magenta* only for a narrow pink-purple band. Desaturate a color far enough and it gets renamed: lime becomes "green", yellow becomes "olive", pink becomes "purple". ⚠ check against Mylonas and MacDonald's published data. ([alrdClpGHvY @6:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=alrdClpGHvY&t=365s))
4. **Webster's Third (1961) defined colors the way ColorHub does.** Each of its 3,000+ color entries was a core ISCC-NBS category plus comparisons to three other named colors in directional words: *bluer*, *lighter*, *stronger*. "Begonia" was a deep pink compared against coral, fiesta and sweet William. The method implies color is a space you move around in. ([anLc2g-6Y1Y @0:31](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=anLc2g-6Y1Y&t=31s), [@8:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=anLc2g-6Y1Y&t=495s))
5. **The hidden author.** I.H. Godlove designed that system and died partway through. His wife Margaret Godlove, a trained chemist pushed out of industry when she married, did most of the measuring and defining (thousands of entries in about seven months), then co-authored the Munsell glossy standard. ⚠ verify in Kory Stamper's *True Color* (Knopf, 2026). ([anLc2g-6Y1Y @10:49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=anLc2g-6Y1Y&t=649s))
6. **ISCC-NBS** (work from 1931 to about 1970) tried to standardize plain-English color names. It pulled names from about 13 trade sources (stamps, interior design, botany); Maerz and Paul's *Dictionary of Color* (1930) was one. ([anLc2g-6Y1Y @2:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=anLc2g-6Y1Y&t=156s))
7. **Japan made "tone" the unit.** PCCS (Japan Color Research Institute, 1964) took Munsell and grouped each hue plane into named tones, so designers combine tones as well as hues. Its basis: 1960s studies where people rated colors on adjective pairs (heavy/light, calm/exciting, hard/soft); lightness and chroma merged into one felt dimension. Kobayashi's Color Image Scale (founded 1966) maps palettes on warm/cool by soft/hard. The popular claim that PCCS comes from Heian *kasane no irome* layering has no academic support. ([IkMsZ5YFEyo @5:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IkMsZ5YFEyo&t=317s), [@5:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IkMsZ5YFEyo&t=355s))

**Harmony, contrast and mood**
8. **Character beats hue for mood.** In Ellen Divers' research, pale, muted or dark palettes read as calm whatever the hue; "blue is calm, red is exciting" is mostly a chroma and lightness effect. ⚠ verify the study. ([lXDCQjeM8Hk @10:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lXDCQjeM8Hk&t=612s))
9. **The same angle on the wheel is not the same contrast.** Yellow against blue-violet has a far bigger lightness gap than orange against azure, though both are "complements". ([lXDCQjeM8Hk @4:37](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lXDCQjeM8Hk&t=277s))
10. **Legibility lives in lightness.** If a palette reads in grayscale, it works; same-lightness colors vanish for many color-blind viewers. Use a perceptual picker (OKLCH), not HSL/HSB. ([lXDCQjeM8Hk @11:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lXDCQjeM8Hk&t=673s))
11. **Harmony as a path.** A "string" through color space, e.g. step down one lightness level and over two hues, repeatedly (pale yellow-orange, salmon, … dark plum), moves hue, lightness and chroma together. ([ERb1x0MJQvM @7:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ERb1x0MJQvM&t=461s))
12. **Goethe's edge colors are nature's readable gradients.** Look through a prism at a black-white edge: one side runs black, red, orange, yellow, white; the other black, indigo, blue, cyan, white. Hue and lightness shift together in a helix, and the two helixes are complementary through the center. Data-viz maps like viridis, magma and cubehelix converged on the same shape. ([qiXHiABcl-I @0:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qiXHiABcl-I&t=30s), [@5:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qiXHiABcl-I&t=310s))

**Complements, mixing and the RYB myth**
13. **RYB spacing is perceptually wrong.** Red and yellow sit close together; the yellow-to-blue stretch holds a huge range of greens; orange is a thin wedge. ([ERb1x0MJQvM @5:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ERb1x0MJQvM&t=339s), [lXDCQjeM8Hk @5:37](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lXDCQjeM8Hk&t=337s))
14. **Your eye's complements differ from the painter's.** Visual (afterimage, light) opposites are red–cyan, yellow–indigo/blue-violet, green–magenta. With real paints: cadmium and quinacridone reds sit opposite cobalt teal, and chrome oxide green's opposite is a purple, not red. ([alrdClpGHvY @1:32](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=alrdClpGHvY&t=92s), [qQHWBE4ngBA @5:11](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qQHWBE4ngBA&t=311s))
15. **You can mix blue.** Tints of phthalo green and dioxazine purple mix through a (low-chroma) blue, so "primaries can't be mixed" is false. ([qQHWBE4ngBA @6:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qQHWBE4ngBA&t=404s))
16. **Paint mixes travel in curves.** Quinacridone magenta plus white drifts toward purple; blues plus white drift toward cyan; a yellow plus a deep blue goes green in paint but gray in naive digital mixing (Kubelka-Munk, the model behind Mixbox). ([alrdClpGHvY @3:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=alrdClpGHvY&t=214s))
17. **Why mixes go muddy.** Mixes that cross the boundaries at ideal yellow, ideal magenta or ideal cyan bend inward and lose chroma. Transparency matters too: opposing transparent pigments head toward black, opposing opaque ones toward gray. ⚠ the wheel-maker's name is garbled in the captions. ([jQmh1ZX9Wss @0:32](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jQmh1ZX9Wss&t=32s), [@2:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jQmh1ZX9Wss&t=124s))
18. **Red has been a double primary since the start.** d'Aguilon drew RYB as a mixing diagram (1613) and admitted in the same book that orange needs a yellowish red (minium) and purple a bluish one (carmine lake). The full split-primary theory appears in Parkhurst (1898). The channel traces the classroom version to 1930s California watercolorists (Rex Brandt's 1948 wheel is the earliest it found). ⚠ the California link is the creator's own research; credit it if used. ([bT7NNuENBOQ @1:33](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bT7NNuENBOQ&t=93s), [@4:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bT7NNuENBOQ&t=279s))

**Warm and cool**
19. **Temperature is not a dimension of color.** It describes context-dependent shifts in hue and chroma. The same gray reads cool or warm depending on its neighbors; a "cool red" is just purpler and a "warm red" oranger. Green (a hump in the middle of the spectrum) and purple (a dip) are neither warm nor cool. ([_wRA-XvgFKo @1:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_wRA-XvgFKo&t=62s), [WQYLfwa2lAI @1:02](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/WQYLfwa2lAI))
20. **"Warm light, cool shadow" needs two lights.** With one lamp there's no opposite temperature in the shadow; outdoors the sun (warm) and the sky (cool fill) supply both. Where a warm and a cool light overlap you get a faint pink, because the middle (green) wavelengths are short. Visible in Cecilia Beaux's *Twilight Confidences* (1888). ([_wRA-XvgFKo @3:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_wRA-XvgFKo&t=182s), [@6:07](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_wRA-XvgFKo&t=367s))
21. **Veins aren't blue.** They sample as a dull version of the skin's own hue; the vivid surrounding skin pushes them toward blue (simultaneous contrast). ([_wRA-XvgFKo @4:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_wRA-XvgFKo&t=245s))
22. **Warm/cool was invented, then frozen.** Du Fresnoy (c. 1650s) noticed Venetian habits that likely came from their pigments (vivid reds and yellows, few vivid blues). Reynolds spread it in the 1770s; Gartside (1805) split the circle; Hayter (1813) drew the first wheel cut into two temperature buckets. It's chroma, not warmth, that makes colors advance. ⚠ verify dates. ([hIqxSFU8HBc @0:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hIqxSFU8HBc), [@2:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hIqxSFU8HBc&t=121s))

**Seeing and measuring**
23. **Color vision is "wavelength imbalance detection".** Three overlapping cone types each report only *how much* they're stimulated, so wavelengths themselves have no color. A balanced spectrum reads as white. ([u_tQ4UkIPfU @5:07](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=u_tQ4UkIPfU&t=307s))
24. **The Abney effect:** blues of constant wavelength drift toward purple as they get paler (visible in the 1957 DIN plant atlas). ([VGCnpvhEWcA @2:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VGCnpvhEWcA&t=154s))
25. **CIELAB in one minute.** CIE 1931 distances didn't match what we see (greens bunch up, blues spread out); CIELAB (1976) stretched the map so equal distance means roughly equal difference, though blues are still off. The stars in L\*a\*b\* separate it from Hunter's 1948 Lab. ([7BXFFT0aPpE @5:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7BXFFT0aPpE&t=306s))

## 5. Eye Gym drill ideas and how the staircase works

**Shared staircase design.** Use a 2-down/1-up rule (two right in a row makes it harder, one miss makes it easier), which settles near 71% correct. Step size halves after each of the first four reversals, then stays fixed; the score is the average of the last six reversals. Measure in perceptual units: CIEDE2000 for "any difference", and separate ΔL\*, ΔC\* and ΔH\* (or OKLCH equivalents) for axis drills, so the eye score can say "you see lightness to 1.5, chroma to 3, hue to 2.5" per family. That 3-axis profile is the honest version of the eye score and matches the channel's whole argument that color has three dimensions.

| Drill | How it works | Staircase variable (start → floor) | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cross-hue lighter or darker** | Two different hues side by side; tap the lighter. Grayscale reveal after each answer. | ΔL\* 20 → 1 | Value across hues is the hardest and most useful value skill. |
| **Vivid-looks-lighter traps** | Every 5th card is a vivid color next to a duller one of the *same* measured lightness; the honest answer is "same". | Chroma gap of the trap pair 40 → 10 | Trains against the Helmholtz-Kohlrausch effect (vivid colors look lighter). |
| **Which way?** (from idea 1) | Two named colors; answer bluer/redder, lighter/darker, stronger/weaker for one axis. | Size of the difference on that axis 15 → 1.5 | Builds the directional vocabulary the Learn lines use. |
| **Name boundary** (idea 2) | A color steps along one axis (lime toward gray); tap when the name changes. | Not a staircase: plot the crossing over many trials and report the 50% point vs the crowd's. | Shows names as regions and gives a shareable result. |
| **Same hue?** | Reference blue, plus three variants at other lightness/chroma; pick the one with the *same* hue. Abney-prone blues and purples appear more often. | ΔH 12 → 1 | Trains hue constancy across tints and shades. |
| **More vivid?** | Two colors of the same hue but different lightness; pick the one with more chroma. | ΔC\* 20 → 2 | Separates chroma from lightness, the most common confusion. |
| **Afterimage hunt** (idea 6) | Stare 15 s, then pick the ghost color from three close cyan/teal options. | Spacing between the three options (ΔE 15 → 4) | Your own eye shows the true complement. |
| **Same gray, two worlds** (idea 11) | Two patches on clashing grounds; same or different? | True ΔE between the patches 10 → 0.5 | Scores how much context fools you, and trains against it. |
| **Light mixer** (idea 4) | Match the swatch with three lights. | Light step size: halves → quarters → eighths; then a timer | Additive mixing intuition. |
| **Will it go muddy?** (idea 7) | Predict a two-paint mix from three swatches. | ΔE between the three candidate swatches 20 → 5 | Mixing prediction, the painter's daily guess. |
| **Tone sort** | Six chips from one hue plane; drag each into pale, light, vivid, muted, deep or dark. | Number of chips and closeness of the tone zones | Learns the tone words from the hue-plane map. |

Keep all of these in the Gym. None belongs in the flashcard loop, per the product rules.

## 6. What ColorHub could do better (judged against the channel)

1. **The complementary colors page contradicts itself.** The text says staring at red leaves a *blue-green* ghost, but the swatches pair red with a plain green (`#2E9A4F`), blue with orange and yellow with violet, labeled "its opposite". Show two rows: the painter's wheel pairs (red–green, blue–orange, yellow–violet) and the eye's pairs (red–cyan, yellow–blue-violet, green–magenta). Also, "mixed as paint they cancel toward a muddy grey-brown" is too simple: opposing transparent paints go toward black, opaque ones toward gray. (`data/wiki-nodes.js`, `complementary-colors`)
2. **The color wheel page presents warm/cool halves as a feature.** That split dates from Hayter's 1813 wheel; green and purple are neither warm nor cool. Add what a wheel is *for* (predict mixes, choose harmonies, map positions) and where the RYB wheel fails (red and yellow are much closer than 120 degrees). The swatches label red, yellow and blue as "primary, paint" with no caveat. Also check the Moses Harris date: we say about 1766; the channel's video title says 1769. (`color-wheel`)
3. **The warm and cool page lists green and violet as cool,** and calls crimson "a cool red". Per the channel, crimson is better described as *purpler* than vermilion. Add the two-light explanation for warm light and cool shadow, and the history (Du Fresnoy to Hayter). The page already says temperature is relative, which is good. (`warm-and-cool`)
4. **The harmony page is already good** (Schloss and Palmer, "vary value more than hue"). Add the character-first or tone-first view (PCCS tones, the ISCC Color Literacy Project's pale/vivid/muted/dark) and replace the red-yellow-blue triad swatches with a demonstration: three "valid" triads that look bad next to one tone-matched palette that looks good.
5. **"Brighter" is ambiguous in our neighbor lines.** `tools/check.js` accepts "brighter" if *either* lightness or chroma is higher, so a line like Violet's "far brighter than indigo" doesn't say which. Use Godlove's split: *lighter/darker* for lightness, *stronger/weaker* (or *more vivid/duller*) for chroma. Keep "softer" but define it as lower chroma. Avoid "warmer/cooler" as a neighbor direction, since temperature depends on context.
6. **The eye score as one CIEDE2000 number hides the three axes.** Track lightness, chroma and hue thresholds separately per family (§5).
7. **The home map is a flat a\*b\* plane, and CIELAB distorts blues.** Consider plotting in OKLab, and add a lightness view or a per-family hue-plane slice so the 3D nature of color shows. The map shows hue and chroma only, so a pale and a dark color of the same hue sit on top of each other.
8. **Names vs everyday usage.** Learn teaches chartreuse, violet and magenta, which is right for the designer tier, but the lines could note what most people say ("most people would call this lime"). Separately, our CSS chartreuse (`#7FFF00`) is greener than the liqueur's traditional yellow-green; worth a note.
9. **The dark surround (`#121212`).** This is my inference, not the channel's: a near-black surround makes swatches look lighter and more vivid than they would on paper (simultaneous contrast). For lightness drills specifically, a mid-gray surround would be fairer. Worth an A/B test on David's phone.
10. **What we already do right, per the channel:** neighbor-based definitions, a neutral surround, CIEDE2000 gates instead of RGB distance, hex flagged as screen approximations (Bartneck makes the same point about LEGO's on-screen colors), and the myth-busting stance in stories.

## Appendix: per-video notes

- **What Japan Did With Munsell** ([IkMsZ5YFEyo](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IkMsZ5YFEyo)): splits color thinking into *lore* (cultural, prescriptive), *science* (perception, physics) and *theory* (generative tools for makers). Japan's PCCS (Japan Color Research Institute, 1964) took Munsell and added named **tones** (vivid, bright, soft, grayish, light, deep…) so designers combine *tones* as well as hues. Origin: 1960s studies where people rated colors on adjective pairs (heavy/light, calm/exciting, hard/soft); lightness and chroma clustered into one felt dimension (~5:55). Kobayashi's Color Image Scale (NCD, 1966) maps combinations on warm/cool by hard/soft (~6:27). Debunks the claim that PCCS comes from Heian *kasane no irome* layering (~5:17). Munsell became Japan's national standard in 1958 (~8:00, verify).
- **Color Wheels are Flat...Color Isn't** ([ERb1x0MJQvM](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ERb1x0MJQvM)): the Kolormondo globe as a teaching tool. Globe metaphor: white north pole, black south pole, vivid hues on the equator, grays in the core (~1:31). History: Runge's sphere 1810, Munsell's hand-painted globes ~1905, Itten's Farbenkugel 1921 (~1:00). Seen in 3D, red and yellow sit close together and the blue-to-yellow stretch holds a huge green region; orange is a small wedge (~5:39). Ultramarine plus a transparent yellow cuts through the inside and dips darker, giving olives, not lime or emerald (~6:40). Harmony as a "string": step down one lightness level and over two hues, repeatedly, from pale yellow-orange to salmon to dark plum (~7:41).
- **You're Wrong about Color Harmony** ([lXDCQjeM8Hk](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lXDCQjeM8Hk)): every hue peaks in chroma at a different lightness (magenta mid, lime near the top, indigo around L 30 to 40), so hue planes have different shapes (~0:00). Three schools of harmony: hue-first (rotate a template; Goethe's "adds up to the whole circle", Chevreul's analogous 1830s), roles-around-a-key-color (Ritter 2011, Hal Reed 1980s "discord" dots), and the modern **character-first** view (~7:41). Same "complement" angle gives very different contrast: yellow vs blue-violet is a far bigger lightness gap than orange vs azure (~4:37). RYB wheels put red/yellow/blue 120 degrees apart, but perception puts red and yellow much closer (~5:37). Character terms from the Color Literacy Project (Inter-Society Color Council): pale, vivid, muted, dark, defined relative to each hue plane (~9:11). Ellen Divers' research: pale, muted or dark palettes read as calm regardless of hue; arousal tracks chroma and lightness, not hue (~10:12, verify). Practical rule: check legibility in grayscale; use a perceptual picker like OKLCH instead of HSL/HSB (~11:13).
- **The Color Wheel That Couldn't Wait** ([VGCnpvhEWcA](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VGCnpvhEWcA)): shot in a botanical garden with Ernst Biesalski's 1957 *Pflanzenfarben Atlas* (plant color atlas), made so botanists and breeders could hold a leaf or flower to a chip and name its color (~0:00). Uses Manfred Richter's DIN 6164 system (framework 1953; printed chip atlas only from 1961, so Biesalski's atlas was the first printed debut, ~1:01). Richter re-spaced Ostwald's 24 hues by having hundreds of subjects arrange 120 samples into visually even steps (~1:32). The **Abney effect**: blues of constant dominant wavelength drift purple as they lighten (~2:34). Last DIN update 1980, last chips printed ~1983.
- **Color Theory's Dogma Problem** ([qQHWBE4ngBA](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qQHWBE4ngBA)): the 12-hue RYB wheel is a "sigil", cultural shorthand more than information (~1:04). A color wheel has three jobs: predict mixtures, choose harmonies, map positions in color space (~3:38). A good chart is a map: it answers *which* red is opposite *which* green. The classroom reds (cadmium red, quinacridone red, alizarin masstone) sit opposite cobalt teal; the opposite of chrome oxide green is a purple, not red (~5:11). Mixing a phthalo green tint with a dioxazine purple tint passes through blue, so "you can't mix blue" is false (~6:44). Yellows sit far from blues, so for lime green mix yellow with a nearer pigment (~7:14).
- **How to Use My Science-Based Color Wheel** ([alrdClpGHvY](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=alrdClpGHvY)): visual complements are yellow/indigo, red/cyan, green/magenta, not the RYB pairs (~1:32). Complements come from the study of light; "mix complements to get neutral" confuses perceived color with physical pigment (~2:03). Cadmium red (PR108) and cadmium yellow (PY35) sit much closer than 120 degrees (~3:03). Pigments don't mix in straight lines (Kubelka-Munk, the basis of Mixbox): quinacridone magenta plus white drifts toward purple, blues plus white drift toward cyan (~3:34). Prussian blue plus a PR101 maroon gives near-black (~4:35). **Naming:** the disc's name zones follow Dimitris Mylonas's online color-naming survey: people say *lime*, not yellow-green or chartreuse; *purple*, not violet; *magenta* covers only a narrow pink-to-purple band (~6:05). Desaturate lime and it becomes plain "green"; desaturate yellow and it becomes "olive"; desaturate pink and it becomes "purple" (~6:35). Descriptive, not prescriptive naming (~7:06).
- **Goethe's "Edge Colors" are a Design Hack** ([qiXHiABcl-I](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qiXHiABcl-I)): Newton put light through the prism; Goethe looked *through* the prism at a black-white edge (~0:00). One edge shows black to indigo to blue to cyan to white; the other black to red to orange to yellow to white. Hue and lightness shift together (~0:30). Plotted in 3D, each series is a looping helix; the two loops are complementary through the center: dark indigo vs light yellow, cyan vs red, azure vs orange (~2:33). A readable gradient palette survives grayscale, keeps lightness contrasts for common color-vision deficiencies, and has perceptually even steps (~3:04). Viridis and magma (matplotlib) converge on the same helix shape (~5:10). Cubehelix with a small rotation (about 0.2 to 0.6) makes good limited color maps (~7:16).
- **How to Predict Paint Mixture Results** ([jQmh1ZX9Wss](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jQmh1ZX9Wss)): the color space has hidden boundaries at ideal yellow, ideal magenta and ideal cyan; mixes that stay inside one section bend outward and keep chroma, mixes that cross a boundary bend inward and go muddy (~0:32, from Doug Weaver and the Mayer/Moriarty-style wheel shown; names in transcript are garbled, verify). Phthalo blue plus quinacridone magenta crosses a boundary and loses some chroma, but far less than cadmium red plus phthalo blue (~1:33). Paint mixing is a compromise between subtractive and optical (averaging) mixing: opposing *transparent* pigments go toward black, opposing *opaque* pigments go toward gray (~2:04).
- **The Quest to Define Colors (Kory Stamper interview)** ([anLc2g-6Y1Y](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=anLc2g-6Y1Y)): Stamper's book *True Color* (Knopf, 2026-03-31). Webster's Third (1961) defined "begonia" as a deep pink compared against three other named colors (coral, fiesta, sweet William) with words like *bluer, lighter, stronger* (~0:31). The ISCC-NBS standard (work from 1931 into the late 1960s) aimed to standardize plain-English color descriptions and compiled names from ~13 trade standards (stamps, interior design, botany), e.g. "M" marks Maerz and Paul's *Dictionary of Color* (1930) (~2:36). I.H. Godlove's system: core ISCC-NBS category plus three "supplemental" comparisons in directional language, implying color is a space you move through (~8:15). Over 3,000 color definitions in the Third, many written in about seven months (~10:18). Godlove died mid-project (burst appendix); his wife **Margaret Godlove**, a trained chemist, did most of the hands-on defining and measurement, then co-authored the Munsell glossy standard (presented ~1957) (~10:49 to 14:53).
- **How Our Eyes Turn Light into Color** ([u_tQ4UkIPfU](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=u_tQ4UkIPfU)): color vision is "wavelength imbalance detection"; a balanced spectrum reads as white (~0:00). Graph-paper exercise: letters A to L stand for photon wavelengths; a yellow block "punches out" the short ones; count what's left to get a reflectance chart (~0:30 to 2:33). Three overlapping cone types (L, M, S) compress the whole spectrum into three numbers; each cone only knows *how much* it is stimulated, not by which wavelength, so wavelengths themselves have no color (~3:03 to 5:07).
- **Warm Light, Cool Shadow... Or Vice Versa?** ([_wRA-XvgFKo](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_wRA-XvgFKo)): temperature is not one of the three dimensions (hue, lightness, chroma); it describes context-dependent hue/chroma shifts. The same gray dot reads cool or warm depending on the palette around it (~1:02). A "cool red" is better called purpler, a "warm red" oranger (~1:32). Single-light still lifes (garlic and potato) show no opposite temperature in the shadows; the warm-light/cool-shadow rule needs *two* light sources, like low sun plus blue skylight outdoors (~2:32 to 3:33). The opposite camp is often fooled by simultaneous contrast: veins look blue but sample as low-chroma skin hue (~4:05). Where warm and cool lights overlap, the result is a slight pink/magenta, because the middle (green) wavelengths are under-represented; visible in Cecilia Beaux's *Twilight Confidences* (1888), Monet, Sorolla (~6:07 to 7:07). Colored bounce light (green lawn, red wall) decides shadow color (~7:37).
- **Where did the Warm/Cool Thing Come From?** ([hIqxSFU8HBc](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hIqxSFU8HBc)): around 1650 Charles-Alphonse Du Fresnoy noticed Venetian painters put reds/yellows in light foregrounds and blues/greens in dark shadows, and spoke of warm vs cold colors (~0:00). Joshua Reynolds spread it in Royal Academy lectures in the 1770s (~0:31). The Venetian habit likely came from available pigments: vivid reds and yellows, few vivid blues and greens (~1:01). Thomas Bardwell (~1750s) claimed reds/yellows advance and blues/greens recede; critics mocked it (the sun is red and very far away) (~1:31). The narrator's point: *chroma*, not temperature, makes colors advance; flip the hues of Monet's *Impression, Sunrise* and the blue sun still pops (~2:01). Gérard de Lairesse tied cool colors to atmospheric perspective (~2:31). Mary Gartside (1805) first split the circle (warm 120 degrees, cold 240 degrees); Charles Hayter (1813) drew the first warm/cold wheel, turning temperature into two abstract buckets (~3:01 to 4:04).
- **The Real Reason Your Palette Has Two of Every Color** ([bT7NNuENBOQ](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bT7NNuENBOQ)): dyers knew since antiquity that three dyes (madder, weld, woad) mix a large range (~0:30). François d'Aguilon (*Opticorum libri sex*, 1613) first drew RYB as a mixture diagram, then admitted no pigment is the ideal red: use minium for orange and carmine lake for purple, so red was "double" from the start (~1:00 to 1:33). A 1708 French text named two primary reds (vermilion-like and crimson/lake-like) (~2:03). George Field (*Chromatography*, 1835) recommended two pigments per color; he was a pigment seller (~2:35). First full double-primary theory in print: Daniel Burleigh Parkhurst, *The Painter in Oil* (1898) (~3:39). The split-primary palette cohered in 1930s California watercolor (Chouinard; Millard Sheets, Phil Dike, Rex Brandt); earliest double-primary wheel found is Brandt's 1948 book (~4:39 to 5:10). Later books contradict themselves (Salem 1970s, Garcia 2006); Grumbacher's 1972 Color Compass framed it as chroma control and avoided warm/cool (~6:44 to 8:16).
- **LEGO Color Wheels (And Why I Built a Grid Instead)** ([7BXFFT0aPpE](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7BXFFT0aPpE)): fan-made LEGO wheels copy Itten's RYB layout or put hues out of order (~0:31). Interview with Christoph Bartneck (University of Canterbury, ex-LEGO, *The Unofficial LEGO Color Guide*): LEGO keeps exact colors a trade secret; its own software's RGB values are inconsistent with real bricks (~2:02). About 50 colors are in current production; the book catalogs 200+ including discontinued ones; warm grays were replaced by bluish grays around 2004 (~3:05 to 4:05). Clear CIELAB explainer: CIE 1931 XYZ distances didn't match visual differences (greens look alike, blues look different at the same distance); CIELAB (1976) squashed and stretched the map so equal distance means roughly equal difference; L* 0 to 100, a* green to red/magenta, b* blue to yellow; the stars distinguish it from Richard Hunter's 1948 Lab (~5:06 to 7:42). The build: a 48x48 baseplate grid with brick towers for lightness, placing ~48 LEGO colors by a*/b* (~7:42 to 10:14). Shows where the LEGO palette has gaps and pile-ups (~11:14). Bartneck's team is building a camera app with a calibration target to identify brick colors (~11:14).
- **Color Theory You Never Learned: Hue Planes** ([Gd1qsQgsE5w](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Gd1qsQgsE5w)): the hue circle is one loop in a 3D solid; a vertical slice is a plane of constant hue (~1:00 to 2:02). Munsell student set: vivid magenta peaks mid-value (5 of 8), green-yellow peaks at value 8; magenta reaches chroma 12, blue only 8 (~2:34 to 3:06). Hue planes are how you *name* variations of one hue: pale/vivid/muted/dark, or ISCC-NBS modifiers (pale, light, brilliant, strong, vivid, moderate, grayish, dark, deep), or PCCS tones (soft, dull, grayish, light grayish) (~3:06 to 3:39). Different ways to grid a hue plane: Munsell (lightness x chroma), saturation lines (a dark color can stay saturated), Hård's NCS-style atlas, DIN, and Roy Berns' vividness (distance from black) and depth (distance from white) (~4:09 to 5:43). RGB cubes have no hue planes (~5:43).
- **RYB vs. CMY Color Wheels** (Short, [LfgswanxffM](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/LfgswanxffM), 3.5M views): a circa-1950 Met "Junior Museum" wheel with transparent red/yellow/blue overlays: red over yellow stays red (hue angle ~357), red over a cyan-ish blue goes nearly black instead of purple (~0:00 to 0:32). The 1980s Process Color Selector wheel (CMY in 10% steps) mixes far better; the *amount* of each primary matters as much as the choice (~1:03).
- **Newly Discovered color, "Olo"** (Short, [MLG5jjPUwTA](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/MLG5jjPUwTA), 340K): explains the 2025 Olo experiment with a Maxwell triangle of cone signals. Monochromatic light walks the edge of the triangle: 700 nm stimulates only L cones; past ~543 nm S cones join (~0:00 to 1:03). A 488 nm laser aimed at mapped individual M cones pushes the signal outside the natural range; spill into neighboring cones means subjects saw something short of a "pure M" color (~1:03 to 2:05).
- **is Green WARM or COOL?** (Short, [WQYLfwa2lAI](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/WQYLfwa2lAI), 114K): warm colors are reflectance curves sloping up toward long wavelengths (red to orange to yellow to white); cool colors slope the other way (violet to blue to cyan to white). Green is a hump in the middle and purple a dip, so both are neither warm nor cool; shift them and they *become* yellow or blue, red or blue (~0:00 to 1:02).
- **Magical Color Theory** (Short, [q6l2-l9e50o](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/q6l2-l9e50o), 283K): red, green and blue flashlights make white; an object casts three colored shadows (cyan, magenta, yellow), and each shadow blocks only its complementary light (~0:00).
- **RGB color mixing guessing game** (Short, [j2cBYZtyrms](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/j2cBYZtyrms)): a quiz format: "which mix of red/green/blue light at full or half power matches this swatch?" Orange = full red + half green; pale periwinkle = full blue + half green + half red; olive = half red + half green; pale fuchsia = red + blue + a little green; gray = half of each (~0:00 to 1:03).
